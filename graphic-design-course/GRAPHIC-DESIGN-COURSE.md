# The Phone-Only Graphic Design Course

**From complete beginner to portfolio-ready in 15 study days — 2 h 30 m a day, Saturday to Wednesday, with Thursday and Friday off.**

You do not need a laptop, a tablet, a Wacom pen or an Adobe subscription. You need a phone, a notebook (paper, yes, really), free apps, and the discipline to show up for fifteen sessions.

By Day 15 you will have: a working design vocabulary, a repeatable process from brief to export, four finished portfolio pieces, one complete capstone brand identity, and a portfolio page you can send to a paying client.

## Read this page before Day 1

### Who this is for

Someone starting from zero who wants to design real things — flyers for a business, social media content for a brand, logos, posters, app screens — and eventually get paid for it. Every lesson assumes no prior knowledge. Nothing assumes a computer.

### The honest limits of a phone (and how we work around them)

| What's harder on a phone | The workaround this course uses |
| --- | --- |
| Precise vector drawing (logos, icons) | Build with geometric shapes and type in Canva/Adobe Express; refine with node-level apps like Vector Ink or Ibis Paint; redraw the final mark cleanly when you get laptop access |
| Large multi-page layouts (brochures, books) | Design single pages/spreads at exact size, not one giant file |
| True print production (CMYK, bleed, 300 dpi) | Design at 300 dpi equivalent, export "PDF Print" with crop marks and bleed from Canva; for real press jobs, a print shop or a laptop hour is the honest answer — Day 10 teaches you to spec it correctly |
| Heavy photo retouching | Layer-based editing in Photopea in the mobile browser; Snapseed for tone and healing |
| Speed | Design small and often. Phone design forces you to think before you tap, which is a skill, not a handicap |

You will be told, day by day, when a task is "phone-ideal" and when it is "phone-possible but better later". No pretending.

### Six rules of practice

1. **Finish bad work.** An ugly finished poster teaches more than a beautiful abandoned one. Every day has a deliverable; ship it.
2. **Ten thumbnails before you open the app.** Two minutes of paper sketches saves forty minutes of dragging boxes around.
3. **One project at a time.** No new file until the current one is exported.
4. **Steal structure, not style.** Study *why* a layout works, then rebuild it with your own content. Copying a finished design teaches you to copy.
5. **Name your files properly.** `day03-poster-v2.png`, not `IMG_20261027`. You will thank yourself on Day 15 when you're assembling a portfolio.
6. **Show the work.** Post one thing per week somewhere public (status, story, group chat). Feedback you can get beats feedback you imagine.

### How you will be marked (self-marking rubric)

Score each deliverable out of 12. You are not marking "do I like it" — you are marking whether it does its job.

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | No clear message | Message exists but buried | Clear message, some noise | One message, instantly readable |
| **Hierarchy** | Everything shouts equally | Some order, weak contrast | Clear first/second/third read | Eye lands exactly where intended |
| **Craft** | Misaligned, inconsistent, cut off | Mostly tidy, visible slips | Aligned, consistent, clean edges | Deliberate spacing, nothing accidental |
| **Fitness** | Wrong size/format for the use | Roughly right | Correct size, safe zones respected | Correct format and ready to publish/print |

**9–12:** move on. **6–8:** redo the weakest criterion tonight, in 20 minutes. **0–5:** redo the deliverable tomorrow morning during Recall — do not build on a broken foundation.

Not secret software tricks. Advanced design is: making decisions on purpose, defending them with reasons, designing systems instead of one-off images, and being able to hand work to a printer, a developer or a client without it breaking. Days 11–15 are entirely about that.

# Day 1 — What graphic design actually is

**Phase:** Foundations · **Week 1, day 1** · **Study slot:** Saturday
**Time:** 2 h 30 m · **Tools today:** Canva, your camera, Notes, Pinterest
**You need:** your phone, a notebook and a pen, and five real designs you can photograph or screenshot

## Why this matters

Almost everyone who says "I want to learn graphic design" means "I want to learn Photoshop". So they spend three months learning menus and produce nothing anyone wants. Design is not software operation. Design is a series of decisions about what to show, how big, in what order, and in what style — to get a specific person to do a specific thing. Software is just how you execute the decisions.

Today you learn the decision-making frame you will use for the next fourteen days, you learn to see like a designer instead of a customer, and you set up the studio you will work in. No beautiful portfolio piece today. That is deliberate.

## By the end of today you can

- Explain the difference between decoration and communication, and say which one a given piece of design is doing.
- Name the five stages of the design process and say what "cheap decisions first" means.
- Break any design into its six usual decision areas: format, structure, type, colour, image, hierarchy.
- Give a critique using observation and reasoning instead of taste ("I like it").
- Run a phone-only workflow: research album, work album, final album, design log.

## Lesson

### 1. Design is a decision under constraints

Every design job has three constraints and they are always the same three: **the audience** (who is this for), **the objective** (what should they think, feel or do), and **the constraints** (budget, size, deadline, brand rules, the fact that it will be seen for 1.5 seconds on a phone).

A designer's whole job is choosing, among infinite options, the ones that serve those three. This is why "make it pop" is not a brief, and why a poster with a beautiful photo and seven messages has failed even if it's pretty.

The three questions to ask about any design, including your own:

1. **Who is it for?** Not "everyone". "Women 25–40 in Kampala who already follow the salon."
2. **What is the one thing they must get?** One. Not three.
3. **What must they do next?** Call, walk in, remember the name, trust the brand.

If you cannot answer all three about your own design, you are decorating, not designing.

### 2. Decoration vs communication

Both are legitimate. Posters for a concert, album covers, wedding invites — these can be expressive first. A hospital form, a bank app screen, a road sign — these must be functional first. Most paid work sits in between: social media content, business flyers, logos, product packaging. The mistake beginners make is being expressive where clarity is needed, and timid where expression is needed.

**Test:** squint at the design. If you can still tell what it's for and what matters most, it communicates. If it becomes a grey smear of equal-weight noise, it doesn't.

### 3. The five-stage process (you will use this every day)

| Stage | What you do | Why it saves you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Brief | Write the audience, objective, constraints, and the one message | Stops you designing for yourself |
| 2. Research | Collect 10–20 references; note *why* each works | Gives you options you'd never invent alone |
| 3. Sketch | 10–20 rough thumbnails on paper, 2 minutes total | Cheap decisions first — a bad idea costs 10 seconds, not 40 |
| 4. Build | Only the 2–3 strongest thumbnails, at real size | You build less and better |
| 5. Test and revise | Squint test, distance test, ask one person, fix | Catches the obvious before the client does |

"Cheap decisions first" is the single most valuable habit in this course. Paper is cheap. A printed wrong flyer is not.

### 4. The six decision areas

When you feel stuck — and you will — ask which of these six is the problem:

1. **Format** — square, portrait, landscape, print size. Wrong format ruins good design.
2. **Structure** — grid, margins, alignment. Is anything lining up?
3. **Type** — which typefaces, what sizes, how much, in what order.
4. **Colour** — how many, how loud, and what job each one does.
5. **Image** — photo, illustration, icon, or nothing. Is it doing work or filling space?
6. **Hierarchy** — the order the eye reads things, created by size, weight, contrast, colour, space and position.

"Make it better" is unactionable. "My hierarchy is flat because headline and body are the same weight" is actionable. This list is how you turn a vague feeling into a fix.

### 5. Your phone studio

Set this up today, properly, because Day 15 depends on it:

- **Three albums in your gallery or Google Drive:** `01-Research`, `02-Work-in-progress`, `03-Final`.
- **A Design Log** — one note (Google Keep, Apple Notes, whatever) with a dated entry per day. Template: *Goal · What I made · What worked · What I'd change · Research for tomorrow.*
- **One Pinterest board** called `GD Course — Research`. Every day you add at least five images and one word about why.
- **Canva home screen** — create a folder `GD Course` and save your first two templates into it.

Rules: every finished file goes to `03-Final` the same day it's finished. A design you can't find is a design that doesn't exist.

### 6. How to critique without taste

Replace "I like it" with three sentences:

- **"I notice…"** — a fact. "I notice the headline is the same weight as the paragraph text."
- **"This works because…"** — a reason. "This works because the price is the only red element, so it's the second thing I read."
- **"This fails because…"** — a reason tied to purpose. "This fails because at thumbnail size the phone number is unreadable, and the whole point is to get calls."

Taste is personal and arguable. Observations and reasons are not. This is also how professionals get paid: clients accept reasoning, not preferences.

> **Try it now (5 min):** open Canva, pick any template, and write one "I notice / works because / fails because" about it before reading further.

## Guided practice

Work in this order. Times are targets, not laws.

1. **(10 min) Collect five designs you meet today.** A shop poster, a bank or mobile-money app screen, a church or event flyer, a WhatsApp status ad, a product label. Screenshot or photograph each. Put them in `01-Research`.
2. **(15 min) Break each one into the six decision areas.** In your Design Log, one line each: format / structure / type / colour / image / hierarchy. For hierarchy, write the actual order your eye moved — "logo → price → phone number → everything else".
3. **(10 min) Answer the three questions for each.** Who is it for, what is the one thing, what should they do next. Mark each one **clear** or **muddy**. Muddy is normal. Most local design fails on question 2.
4. **(10 min) Squint test.** For each of the five, half-close your eyes. Note which still communicate.
5. **(10 min) Set up the studio.** Albums, Design Log, Pinterest board, Canva folder. Do it now, not later.
6. **(10 min) Your first sketch.** Pick the muddiest of the five. In Canva, on a blank A4 portrait page, rebuild its *structure* in 15 minutes using only black rectangles and grey text. No colour, no photos, no fonts beyond Canva's default. Then compare: you'll see that most designs are three or four boxes and a hierarchy.

## Practical questions

Answer in writing, in your Design Log. Three or four sentences each. Answers are at the end of the day.

1. A client says: "I want a poster that tells people we're open 8am–8pm, we deliver, we're the cheapest, and we have new stock." What is the first thing you fix, and what do you ask them?
2. Why is "cheap decisions first" a rule and not just a preference? Give an example of an expensive decision.
3. Look at your five collected designs. Which one has the strongest hierarchy, and *what specifically* creates it? Name at least two tools used (e.g. size, weight, colour, space, position).
4. Is a wedding invitation a communication problem or a decoration problem? Defend your answer.
5. What is the difference between a client's taste and a client's objective, and why does conflating them cost you money?
6. Your design looks good on your phone but a client says "I can't read it". What are the three most likely causes?

## Scenario

**The nine-font flyer.** A friend runs a small hardware shop and brings you a flyer made in a hurry: nine fonts, four colours that clash, a stock photo of a power drill, three phone numbers, the shop name in Comic-style lettering, and a WhatsApp screenshot pasted in the corner. She says: "People say it's too busy. Can you just make it nice?"

You have 15 minutes and your phone.

Write a **diagnosis note** of no more than 10 lines:

- The one message this flyer should carry (you choose, and you justify it).
- The three specific things that make it fail, using the six decision areas as your vocabulary.
- What you would remove, and what you would keep.
- One question you'd ask the owner before redesigning anything.

Do **not** redesign it today. Diagnosing is the skill; rebuilding comes from Day 3.

- [ ] Five designs in `01-Research`
- [ ] Five breakdown notes (six decision areas + three questions + squint test)
- [ ] Studio set up: three albums, Design Log, Pinterest board, Canva folder
- [ ] One 15-minute black-and-grey structural sketch
- [ ] The diagnosis note for the nine-font flyer

## Answers and self-marking

1. **Fix the message, not the layout.** Four messages is zero messages. Ask: "If a customer remembers only one of those four, which one pays your bills?" Then build the hierarchy around that one and demote the rest to small supporting text. Asking is the answer; guessing is the mistake.
2. Cheap decisions first because mistakes get more expensive as the process advances. A layout mistake caught in a thumbnail costs 10 seconds; caught in a printed run of 500 flyers costs the print bill plus the client's trust plus the reprint deadline. Expensive decision: choosing paper stock and print quantity before the layout is approved.
3. There is no single right answer — marks are for naming *mechanisms*. Good answers mention at least two of: size contrast, weight contrast, colour isolation, white space around the focal element, position (top-left or centre), or the removal of competing elements. "It's cleaner" is not a mechanism.
4. Defensible either way, but a strong answer: it's communication *and* decoration, and the objective is emotional — the guest must feel the tone of the event before reading a date. That means expression is legitimate, but the date, venue and names still have to be findable in three seconds. Decoration with a functional core.
5. Taste is "I don't like green". Objective is "customers must see the discount price". Taste is negotiable and subjective; objective is measurable and is what you're being paid to serve. If you treat taste as objective you'll do endless free revisions. If you treat objective as taste you'll lose the client.
6. In order of likelihood: (a) contrast — grey text on a coloured photo, or light weight at small size; (b) size — body text below roughly 14–16 px equivalent at the viewing distance; (c) too much text competing, so the eye can't settle. Also acceptable: low resolution, and text over a busy image without a scrim.

# Day 2 — Elements of design and how the eye groups things

**Phase:** Foundations · **Week 1, day 2** · **Study slot:** Sunday
**Time:** 2 h 30 m · **Tools today:** Canva, Ibis Paint X (or paper), Pinterest
**You need:** yesterday's five collected designs, your Design Log

## Why this matters

Yesterday you learned the six decision areas. Today you learn the raw materials you make those decisions with — line, shape, form, space, texture, colour, value, type — and the dozen honest rules about how human eyes group and separate them. These rules are not decoration advice; they are perception. Once you know that things close together read as a group, you stop wondering why your five bullet points look like a random pile and start controlling the grouping on purpose.

This is the day that separates people who "have an eye" from people who have vocabulary.

## By the end of today you can

- Name the eight elements of design and identify each in a real piece of work within seconds.
- Use positive/negative space deliberately rather than as leftover pixels.
- Apply five Gestalt principles (proximity, similarity, closure, figure/ground, common fate) to fix a layout.
- Explain value (light/dark) as the strongest hierarchy tool before colour is involved.
- Produce a design using exactly one element, aggressively, without it looking empty.

## Lesson

### 1. The eight elements

Elements are the ingredients. Principles (Day 3) are the cooking methods.

| Element | What it is | What it's good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Line** | Any mark with length and direction — rules, borders, arrows, underlines, hand-drawn strokes | Direction, division, movement, connecting and separating |
| **Shape** | A closed 2D area: circle, square, triangle, blob | Recognisability, icons, containers, mood (circles = soft/friendly, squares = stable/formal, triangles = tension/energy) |
| **Form** | The illusion of 3D — shading, perspective, drop shadow, isometric | Weight, realism, product feel |
| **Space** | The empty area, and the positive/negative relationship | Breathing room, focus, luxury, clarity |
| **Texture** | The look or feel of a surface — grain, paper, fabric, noise, gloss | Warmth, tactility, craft, differentiation |
| **Colour** | Hue, saturation, value | Emotion, attention, brand recognition |
| **Value** | Lightness/darkness, independent of hue | **Hierarchy and legibility** — the most underrated element |
| **Type** | Letterforms as visual material | Voice and information at the same time |

**Try it (3 min):** in Canva, take a blank square. Place one black circle. Move it so it's dead centre; then to the lower-right third; then let it bleed off the edge. Notice how the same shape becomes static, then balanced, then dynamic and a bit uncomfortable. That is position + space doing the work, not the shape.

### 2. Value does more for hierarchy than colour

Squint at your design and colour disappears; value remains. That is why a poster can be gorgeous in colour and unreadable in greyscale — and why print designers check in black and white before approving.

Practical rule: build every design in **black, white and one grey** first. Get the hierarchy right at that stage, then add colour to *support* a hierarchy that already exists. Beginners add colour first and then fight it for an hour.

**Try it (4 min):** in Canva, take your Day 1 structural sketch and change the headline to black, the supporting line to mid-grey, and the details to light grey. Screenshot. The design should now work with no colour at all.

### 3. Space is a material, not an absence

Two kinds:

- **Positive space** — the shapes, type, images.
- **Negative space** — everything else. It is not empty; it is doing one of three jobs: **framing** (pushing attention inward), **separating** (stopping elements from blending), or **signalling** (lots of space reads as premium, confidence, calm; cramped space reads as cheap, urgent, noisy).

The most common beginner error is fear of space. If a layout feels crowded, the fix is almost never "make it smaller" — it's "delete something and let the rest breathe".

**Margin discipline:** nothing important should sit closer than roughly one thumb-width from the edge on a phone, or 5 mm on a small print piece. Canva shows guides; use them.

### 4. Gestalt: how eyes group

Your brain does not see elements one at a time; it sees groups. These are the groupings you can control:

- **Proximity** — things near each other read as related. The single most powerful and most ignored principle. If your caption floats equidistant between two photos, it belongs to neither.
- **Similarity** — things that look alike (same colour, size, shape, style) read as a family. This is how you make five different items feel like one brand.
- **Closure** — the mind completes incomplete shapes. You can draw an implied circle with four arcs and people still see a circle. This is the basis of most clever logos.
- **Figure/ground** — what reads as the subject vs the background. Ambiguity here creates the "vase or two faces" effect: memorable in art, confusing in a price list.
- **Common fate / continuation** — things moving or pointing the same way read as one. That's why arrows and sight-lines in photos steer attention.

**Quick self-test:** find any crowded poster and mentally draw boxes around clusters. If the clusters don't match the intended groups (name + logo, price + product, address + phone), the layout is lying to the reader. Fix it with spacing, not with borders.

### 5. One element, done aggressively

Strong design often picks one element and commits: an entire poster of lines, a logo built from a single circle, a brand that is all paper texture. Commitment reads as confidence. Mixing five elements at medium intensity reads as clip-art.

The trick to "one element" design is that you still need hierarchy — which means you vary the element itself: line weight, line spacing, circle size, texture density. Variation within constraint is the whole game.

> **Try it (5 min):** in Canva, make a 1080×1080 blank. Add 12 lines, all the same colour, and try to create a clear focal point using only line weight and spacing. You'll find it's harder than it looks and more satisfying than a template.

You'll discover that most local design relies almost entirely on photo + type, and that space is usually the missing element.

## Answers and self-marking

1. **Value.** Hierarchy is about contrast, and value contrast survives every condition — bad screens, bright sunlight, greyscale printing, colour-blind viewers, small thumbnails. Colour adds a second, weaker channel on top. A design whose hierarchy depends only on hue collapses the moment it's printed in mono or viewed by someone who can't separate the two hues.
2. **Similarity and proximity, working together.** Put the main logo alone, larger, with space around it; group the other three together, smaller, at lower contrast, in one row or line. Consistency of size and spacing within the group of three makes them read as "supporting credits", and isolation makes the main mark read as "the brand". Adding "in partnership with" text helps semantics, but the grouping must work without it.
3. Cramped says: cheap, urgent, noisy, low confidence, "everything is equally important, so nothing is". Spacious says: confident, considered, premium, "we know what matters, so we left room for it". 4. Figure/ground confusion is when the viewer can't tell subject from background. Failure: white logo on a busy photo — the logo dissolves into the image. Deliberate: a logo that reads as a letter and as an object (FedEx's arrow, the WWF panda forms, Rubin's vase), or a poster where the negative space forms a hidden word. Deliberate ambiguity rewards a second look; accidental ambiguity just costs comprehension.
5. Because commitment produces a consistent visual language. One element varied (weight, scale, spacing, density) creates rhythm and family resemblance, which the eye reads as considered. Five elements at medium strength create competition, which reads as untrained. Constraint is visible.
6. (a) Add a scrim — a dark or light semi-transparent band behind the text; (b) increase value contrast by lightening the text and darkening the area behind it (or move the text to a calmer part of the photo). Also valid: add a solid colour block, or crop the photo so a flat area sits behind the text. Changing the font is the weakest fix because the problem is contrast, not letterforms.

# Day 3 — Composition, alignment and hierarchy

## Why this matters

You can have a good typeface, a great photo and a sensible palette and still produce something that looks amateur — because nothing lines up and everything is the same size. Composition is where design stops being a pile of elements and starts being a message. It is also the cheapest skill to improve: most of the difference between a beginner's poster and a professional's poster is alignment, spacing and one clear focal point.

## Lesson

### 1. Balance: the three options

- **Symmetrical** — mirrored around a centre axis. Feels stable, formal, traditional, trustworthy. Great for institutions, certificates, invitations, luxury. Risk: static and boring if nothing else varies.
- **Asymmetrical** — different weights on each side, balanced like a scale with a big stone on one side and a pile of small ones on the other. Feels modern, dynamic, editorial. Risk: needs judgement, and can look accidental if it's *nearly* balanced.
- **Radial** — everything radiates from a centre. Feels focused and energetic; good for stamps, badges, round logos, event graphics.

**How to check:** step back and squint. Does the design feel like it's tipping over to one side? Cover half of it with your hand — does the visible half feel too light or too heavy? "Almost balanced" is the worst result; commit to one.

### 2. Alignment is a promise

Every edge in your design either aligns with another edge or it should have an obvious reason not to. When nothing aligns, the viewer's brain keeps searching for order and the design feels "off" without anyone being able to say why. That unnamed off-ness is what people mean by "it looks like it was made in a hurry" or "it looks local".

The practical version:

- **Pick one grid and obey it.** Canva: Layout → Guides, or use a template's structure but delete its content. A 3-column grid with a consistent margin covers 90% of beginner work.
- **Left-align bodies of text.** Centre-align only short things — a title, a date, a single-line invitation. Centred paragraphs create a ragged, weak left edge and are hard to read past three lines.
- **Align to a strong line, not to the middle of nothing.** If a design has a strong horizontal rule at the top, everything else wants to relate to it.
- **Optical beats mathematical.** A circle the same width as a square looks smaller. Text with a flat left edge looks indented if the first letter is round (O, C, G). Nudge by eye, then check with guides — designers "cheat" constantly; the trick is cheating in the direction the eye wants.

**Try it (5 min):** in Canva, put a headline, a paragraph, and a button anywhere. Screenshot. Now select all three and align left, then set the same margin. Screenshot. The difference in perceived quality is usually larger than any font choice you'll make.

### 3. Hierarchy in three levels, not seven

A design needs a reader to know, instantly: *what is this, why do I care, what do I do.* That's three levels.

| Level | Purpose | Typical treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Focal | The one thing | Biggest, boldest, highest contrast, most space around it |
| 2 — Support | The reason to care | Medium size/weight, often a different colour or lighter value |
| 3 — Detail | Practical info | Small, grey, tight leading, grouped together |

Everything on the page belongs to a level. If you have five different sizes, you have five claims to attention and therefore no hierarchy. **Three sizes, three weights, three values is plenty for a poster.** Amateur work usually has too *many* distinctions, not too few.

Tools for hierarchy, in order of strength:

1. **Size** — the crudest and most reliable. If it must be seen first, make it clearly bigger. Not 10% bigger; 100% bigger.
2. **Value contrast** — black on white vs grey on white. Free and always works.
3. **Weight** — bold vs regular vs light. Use with restraint; four weights is noise.
4. **Space** — isolate the most important element. White space is an attention multiplier.
5. **Colour** — a single accent colour used on one element is a spotlight. Used on five elements, it's a decoration.
6. **Position** — top, centre, and top-left get read first (for left-to-right readers). Bottom-right is where the eye exits — good for a call to action, bad for a logo.

### 4. The focal point

Every design should have exactly one place the eye lands. Ways to create it:

- **Isolation** — put it alone with space around it.
- **Convergence** — let lines, gazes, arrows and perspective point at it (use photos of people looking toward your subject).
- **Contrast** — it's the only dark thing, the only colour, the only round shape.
- **Scale** — it's absurdly bigger than everything else.

The default mistake is to put everything in the centre at medium size, which produces no focal point at all. Centre is a legitimate place for a focal point *if the element is dominant*. Centre plus medium is the flattest composition available.

### 5. Margins, safe zones and the "four-box" test

- **Margin:** keep a consistent margin on all four sides. On a phone, roughly 5–8% of the width. On print, 5 mm minimum for a simple flyer.
- **Safe zone:** for print, keep text at least 3–5 mm inside the trim; for social, keep text inside the central 80% because platforms crop and overlay UI. For stories/reels, keep the bottom 15% and top 10% free of text.
- **Four-box test:** draw a cross through the middle. Do the four quadrants have different weights but a shared structure? All four identical = static; one empty and three busy = probably unbalanced unless deliberate.

> **Try it (5 min):** open a Canva poster template you like. Turn on guides and rulers. Note the margin it uses, how many type sizes, and where the focal point sits. Templates are free composition lessons — read them instead of just filling them.

# Day 4 — Colour theory you can actually use

**Phase:** Foundations · **Week 1, day 4** · **Study slot:** Tuesday
**Time:** 2 h 30 m · **Tools today:** Canva, Coolors (browser), a contrast checker, Snapseed
**You need:** your grid template from Day 3, and three photos with colours you like

## Why this matters

Colour is the element beginners reach for first and understand last. Two things are true at once: colour is the fastest way to make something feel a certain way, and it is the fastest way to make something unreadable. Most amateur design fails not because the colours are ugly but because there are five of them, they all shout, and the text has vanished into them.

Today is not "the meaning of purple". It is: how to build a palette from nothing, how to keep it to three roles, how to prove it works before you commit, and how to survive printing and accessibility.

## By the end of today you can

- Build a palette from a photo, and from a colour wheel harmony, in under three minutes.
- Assign 60/30/10 roles (dominant, support, accent) and defend which colour gets which job.
- Test any palette in greyscale and against a contrast checker before delivering.
- Avoid the five standard colour failures: too many hues, full saturation everywhere, grey text, colour-only meaning, RGB-only thinking.

## Lesson

### 1. Three dials, not one

Every colour can be described by three independent properties. Learn to move them separately and colour stops being mysterious.

- **Hue** — where it sits on the wheel: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple. Changing hue changes the *mood*.
- **Saturation** — how pure or greyed it is. High saturation shouts; low saturation whispers. Changing saturation changes the *volume*.
- **Value** — how light or dark. Changing value changes *readability and hierarchy*.

The most common beginner palette is five different hues at full saturation and mid value — maximum noise, minimum hierarchy. The professional move is usually: **one hue, varied in value and saturation**, plus one accent. That is why so many good brands look like "three blues and a red".

Try this in Canva's colour picker: choose one colour, then drag only the saturation slider to 50%, then only the value slider. You now have a family that agrees with itself.

### 2. Build a palette from a photograph

The fastest reliable method, and it always produces something that feels designed because a photograph already contains coherent colour:

1. Choose a photo with the mood you want (warm street scene, cool ocean, product shot).
2. In Canva, upload it and use **Colour palette from image** (in the colour picker, "Extract from image"), or in Coolors use the image picker.
3. You get five colours. **You will not use all five.** Pick: the darkest (role: text/dark), the lightest (role: background), one mid-tone (role: surfaces), one saturated colour (role: accent). Discard the rest.
4. Adjust: push the dark darker and the light lighter, because photos usually don't contain an extreme enough pair for text contrast.

This is how you build a palette that looks intentional in ten minutes, without a colour theory lecture.

### 3. Harmonies, and when each is right

| Harmony | How it's built | Feels like | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Monochromatic** | One hue, many values | Calm, confident, premium | Editorial, tech, luxury, anything minimal |
| **Analogous** | Two or three neighbours on the wheel | Harmonious, natural, soft | Wellness, food, lifestyle, illustration |
| **Complementary** | Two opposites | Energetic, loud, high contrast | Sales, sport, kids, calls to action |
| **Split-complementary** | One hue plus the two neighbours of its opposite | Energetic but less crude than complementary | Brands that want punch without cliché |
| **Triadic** | Three evenly spaced hues | Playful, vibrant, childlike | Toys, events, entertainment |
| **Neutral + accent** | Greys/beiges plus one saturated colour | Sophisticated, controlled | The safest professional default |

If you cannot choose, take **neutral + accent**. It is almost never wrong, and it is what most well-paid brand work actually uses.

### 4. The 60/30/10 rule

Assign jobs, not just colours:

- **60% dominant** — usually the background or the largest surface. Quiet. Light, desaturated, or a deep neutral.
- **30% support** — secondary surfaces, image blocks, body text.
- **10% accent** — buttons, prices, the one thing you want noticed. Accent is a spotlight; two spotlights cancel out.

In a poster that often means: cream background (60), photo plus dark text block (30), red price (10). If your accent colour covers a third of the page, it is no longer an accent.

### 5. Test before you commit

Three tests, two minutes total, and they catch nearly every colour disaster:

1. **Greyscale test.** Convert the design to black and white (Canva: filter, or export and use your phone's filter). If hierarchy survives, your value structure is right. If it becomes mush, your colours differ only in hue.
2. **Contrast test.** Put your text and background colours into the WebAIM contrast checker. Body text needs **4.5:1**, large text **3:1**. Grey `#999999` on white is 2.85:1 — fails, and is the most common failure in local design.
3. **Colour-blind test.** Roughly 1 in 12 men cannot separate red from green. Never use colour alone to carry meaning: label it, shape it, or icon it as well.

### 6. The five standard failures

1. **Too many hues.** More than three colours (neutrals excepted) is almost always noise.
2. **Everything at full saturation.** Nothing is loud if everything is loud. Desaturate the background, saturate the one thing that matters.
3. **Grey text on white or on photos.** Unless it's very dark grey on white, it's unreadable in sunlight.
4. **Colour as the only signal.** "Red means closed" fails for a colour-blind viewer and in a black-and-white print.
5. **Designing only in RGB.** Screen colour is emitted light; print is reflected ink. Saturated blues and greens often print duller or shift. If it will be printed, check the print preview and accept that a small shift is normal.

> **Try it (4 min):** open your Day 3 poster. Apply a neutral + accent palette: cream background, near-black text, one saturated accent on the price or the call to action. Screenshot. Compare with the original. The accent should feel like it's pointing.

### 7. A shortcut that beats guessing

Steal the *structure* of a good palette rather than the colours themselves. Find a brand whose mood matches your project; note which role each colour plays (dominant, support, accent, text); then substitute your own hues into those roles. Your palette inherits the logic that made theirs work, without copying their identity.

## Guided practice

2. **(10 min) Assign roles.** For each palette write: dominant / support / accent / text / background. Delete at least one colour from each palette — the extraction always gives you one you didn't need.
3. **(10 min) Fix the extremes.** For each palette, darken the dark and lighten the light until you have a pair that passes 4.5:1 in the contrast checker. Record the hex values that pass.

2. Because photographs usually contain no extreme light or dark — they're mid-tones, because that's what cameras expose for. Without a near-white and a near-black you cannot get text contrast or a clear hierarchy, so you must push the ends further than the photo does.

## Lesson

### 1. The parts that matter (five minutes of anatomy)

| Term | What it is | Why you care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Baseline** | The line letters sit on | Everything aligns to it; ignore it and text looks wobbly |
| **X-height** | Height of a lowercase "x" | Bigger x-height reads better small — matters on phone screens |
| **Cap height** | Height of capitals | Controls how "big" an all-caps heading looks |
| **Ascender / descender** | The tails above and below (b, p) | Sets how much line spacing you need |
| **Counter** | The enclosed space inside o, a, e | Big counters stay readable at small sizes |
| **Weight** | Stroke thickness | Your cheapest hierarchy tool after size |

You do not need to draw letters. You need to know that a font with a large x-height and open counters survives being small — which is most of what "readable on a phone" means.

### 2. The categories, and what each one is for

| Category | Examples | Voice | Use for | Avoid for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Serif** | Playfair, Georgia, Lora | Traditional, trustworthy, editorial | Long text in print, luxury, institutions | Tiny screen text at light weights |
| **Sans-serif** | Inter, Lato, Montserrat, Open Sans | Modern, neutral, clear | UI, signage, body text on screen | Nothing, really — it's the default |
| **Slab** | Roboto Slab, Rockwell | Sturdy, mechanical, confident | Posters, headlines, industrial brands | Long body copy |
| **Script / handwritten** | Great Vibes, Caveat | Personal, romantic, informal | Invitations, accents, signature marks | **Anything functional**: phone numbers, prices, body text |
| **Display** | Bebas Neue, Archivo Black | Loud, characterful | Headlines only, at large sizes | Paragraphs, small text |
| **Monospace** | IBM Plex Mono, Courier | Technical, honest, code-y | Data, captions, tech brands | Long reading |

**The rule that fixes most beginner typography:** one or two families, maximum. If you must choose a script or display face, it gets a *small role* (a headline, a single word), and everything functional goes in a plain sans.

### 3. Type scale: three sizes, not ten

Professional layouts use a small set of related sizes. Pick three levels and stick to them:

- **Display / headline:** 2–3× the body size. Big enough to be a gap, not a nudge.
- **Body:** the workhorse. 16–18 px on screen, 9–11 pt in print.
- **Caption / legal:** one step down, often greyed, never smaller than ~11 px on screen.

Everything on the page must be one of the three. When a designer says a layout is "messy", it usually has six sizes. A useful test: list every font size on the page. More than four is a decision you haven't made.

For emphasis inside body text, use **weight** or **value**, not a fourth size.

### 4. Setting text so people can read it

- **Measure (line length):** 45–75 characters per line for body text; on a phone, 30–40. If a line runs the full width of an A4 page, it's too long — split into columns or shorten.
- **Line height (leading):** roughly 1.4–1.6× the font size for body. Tighter for big headlines (1.05–1.2 is fine at display sizes — they need *less* space, not more).
- **Alignment:** left-align anything longer than three lines. Centre short lines only.
- **All-caps:** use for a few words at most, and add slight letter-spacing (about 5–10%) because capitals have no natural spacing rhythm. All-caps paragraphs are measurably slower to read.
- **Tracking/kerning:** tighten very large headlines slightly; never tighten body copy. If a headline's last letter looks like it's falling off the edge, add a hair of space — optical alignment again.
- **Justified text:** avoid on a phone. Justification needs hyphenation and column width to avoid rivers of white space; without them it looks like a ransom note.
- **Contrast:** dark grey (#333) on white reads more comfortably than pure black, but only if it's still ≥ 4.5:1. Mid-grey body text is the most common readability failure.

**Try it (4 min):** in Canva, take one sentence of body text and set it at 16 px with 1.5 line height. Screenshot. Then set the same sentence at 16 px with 1.0 line height, and centred, all-caps. Screenshot. Keep the first one; the second is your reference for "what not to do".

### 5. Pairing typefaces

Pairing is about **contrast of role**, not similarity of style. Two geometric sans-serifs look like a mistake; a high-contrast serif with a neutral sans looks intentional.

Three reliable formulas:

1. **Serif headline + sans body** — the safest, most editorial pairing. E.g. Playfair Display + Inter.
2. **Bold sans headline + regular sans body, same family** — the most modern and the hardest to get wrong. E.g. Montserrat Bold + Montserrat Regular.
3. **Display headline + neutral sans body** — for posters and social. E.g. Bebas Neue + Lato.

And one near-universal rule: **if the two fonts are the same width, weight and mood, you've got one font with extra steps.** Contrast in *style*, not in *file*.

### 6. Matching type to brand

Type carries voice before a single word is read. Ask: is this brand **rational** (bank, clinic, logistics — go neutral sans), **human** (school, café, NGO — humanist sans or soft serif), **modern** (tech, studio — geometric sans), or **expressive** (fashion, events, food — high-contrast serif, display, or a careful script accent)? Pick one, then use the scale and setting rules to keep it disciplined.

> **Try it (5 min):** in Google Fonts, type the name of your capstone brand in four different families — one serif, one geometric sans, one humanist sans, one display. Screenshot them stacked. Choose one and write one sentence on why it fits the audience.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Because functional text must be read at a glance and at distance, and script faces have low legibility: thin joins, decorative swashes, inconsistent x-height, and letters that look alike (a/o, r/n). A script face also signals informality and luxury, which fights the message "call this number now". Use script for a single expressive word, if at all.

3. Leading is the vertical space between lines (line height); tracking is the horizontal space across a run of letters (letter-spacing). 4. ## Lesson

### 1. The grid: margins, columns, gutters

A page has three numbers that decide everything else:

- **Margin** — the white frame. On print, 10–15 mm for A4; on screen, 5–8% of the width.
- **Columns** — how many vertical bands the text sits in. Two or three for A4; one for a phone screen.
- **Gutter** — the space between columns. Usually 4–6 mm on print, roughly one line-height on screen.

Everything else — images, headlines, captions — either aligns to the columns or deliberately spans across them. That "spanning" is what makes a spread look designed: a headline that crosses all three columns, a photo that bleeds off one edge, a pull quote that occupies column three only.

**Set the grid first, in guides, before any content.** If you build content first, you will spend the rest of the day nudging things that almost align.

### 2. Body text: the four settings that matter

1. **Measure** — 45–75 characters. In a two-column A4, at 10 pt, that's usually 60–70 mm column width. In Canva, resize the text box, don't scale the text.
2. **Leading** — 1.4–1.6× for body at 9–11 pt. Add a little more if the typeface has long ascenders/descenders.
3. **Alignment** — left-aligned, ragged right. Justified text only if you control hyphenation and have a wide measure; on a phone, never.
4. **Contrast and weight** — dark grey (not light grey), regular weight, no all-caps, no italics for more than a phrase.

**Try it (4 min):** in Canva, put 150 words of real text into a box that's 60 mm wide at 10 pt, line height 1.5. Screenshot. Now widen the box to full page width. Screenshot. The first is readable; the second is a wall. This is the single most common print-design error in local work.

### 3. Widows, orphans, rivers, runts

- **Widow** — a single word (or very short last line) stranded at the end of a paragraph.
- **Orphan** — the first line of a paragraph stranded alone at the top of a column or page.
- **River** — a vertical gap of white space running down through several lines, usually caused by justification or double spaces.
- **Runt** — a very short last line, widowed or not.

Fixes: rephrase slightly (best), adjust tracking by a hair, adjust the text box height, or rewrite the sentence. Do **not** fix a widow by adding a manual line break that leaves a giant gap — that's more visible than the widow.

### 4. The editorial furniture

These are the elements that make long text look *published* rather than typed:

| Element | Job | Restraint rules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Deck / standfirst** | One or two lines that sell the piece before the body | Set 1.5× body size, lighter value, max 2 lines |
| **Pull quote** | A sentence lifted out, set large, often in a column or spanning one | Use once per spread; it must be a real sentence from the text |
| **Drop cap** | A large first letter | Only on the opening paragraph, only once |
| **Caption** | Explains an image | Smaller than body, grey, sits closer to its image than to the next one |
| **Byline / kicker** | Who and what | Small caps or tracked caps, above the headline |
| **Rule (line)** | Separates blocks | One or two; more starts to look like a form |
| **Sidebar** | Secondary info in a tinted box | One per spread |

The temptation is to use all of them at once. Pick **two** per spread. Restraint reads as confidence.

### 5. Designing for people who won't read it

Most real documents are not read; they are scanned by someone in a hurry, possibly in poor light, possibly with limited literacy in the language used. So:

- **Front-load.** Put the most important thing first, in the biggest type.
- **Chunk.** Short paragraphs, clear subheads, numbered steps.
- **Use icons as well as words** — never instead of words, unless the icon is universally understood (a phone, a clock).
- **One idea per line** where possible, especially in forms and instructions.
- **Test at the distance it will be used.** A leaflet on a clinic wall is read from 1.5 m; a menu is read at 40 cm; an A4 poster on a matatu window is read at 3 m in motion.

### 6. Phone-only production notes

Long documents on a phone are genuinely harder. Practical approach:

- Design **spreads and pages as separate files**, not one long document. Canva's A4/A5 page templates handle this well.
- Use **two columns maximum** on A4 in Canva on a phone — three columns becomes untouchable at that zoom.
- Turn on **guides and rulers**, and set the same margin value on every page, every time. Write the numbers in your log so you can't drift.
- Type in a separate note first, then paste. Editing 300 words inside a tiny text box on a phone is slower than writing it properly once.

5. Because a pull quote's job is to let a skimmer sample the *actual* argument, which builds trust; inventing a marketing line turns the page into an advertisement and duplicates the headline's job. It also creates a credibility problem if a reader reads the body and finds the quote isn't there.

# Day 7 — Phone photography, editing and compositing

## Lesson

### 1. Light before editing

No edit rescues bad light. Three rules cover almost every phone photo you'll take for design work:

- **Use one light source.** A window on one side, no ceiling bulb on, no phone torch mixing with daylight. Mixed colour temperatures (warm bulb + cool daylight) produce a colour cast you cannot fully fix later.
- **Diffuse and bounce.** Direct sun makes hard shadows and blown highlights. Put a white sheet of paper or a thin curtain between the sun and the subject; put another white sheet on the opposite side to bounce light back into the shadows. That's a two-light studio for zero money.
- **Get low, get close, get level.** For products, shoot square-on at the product's height — not looking down from standing. Perspective errors are the most common reason amateur product photos look amateur.

For portraits: window light at 45°, subject facing slightly away from it, background at least a metre behind (so it softens), and focus on the eyes.

### 2. Shoot for the design, not for the subject

Before you press the shutter, answer:

- **Where will the type go?** Leave an area of calm, low-detail space. If you plan to put a headline across the top, don't fill the top with detail.
- **What crop will you need?** Square for feed, 9:16 for stories, wide for a banner. Shoot a little wider than you need so you can crop.
- **What's the smallest it will be seen?** A product shot that will be a thumbnail needs a strong silhouette and high contrast, not fine detail.
- **What's the colour story?** The photo must belong to your palette. If your palette is warm neutrals, don't shoot on a blue plastic table.

### 3. Edit in the right order

The order matters because each step depends on the one before it. Doing it out of order is why edits look "overcooked".

1. **Crop and straighten** (Snapseed: Crop; then Rotate/Straighten). Decide the aspect ratio for the destination.
2. **Exposure** — brightness first, then highlights and shadows. Recover blown highlight areas (they are gone forever if pure white; check with the histogram).
3. **White balance** — remove colour casts so whites are white. Warm/cool and tint sliders.
4. **Contrast and black point** — a photo with no true black looks washed out; set the black point using Curves so the darkest area is nearly black.
5. **Local adjustments** — Snapseed's Selective tool for one area (brighten the product, darken the background), or a radial/vignette mask.
6. **Retouch** — Healing for dust, scratches, blemishes, small distractions. Remove objects that fight the design, not every imperfection; a photo of real food should look like food.
7. **Sharpen and export** — a little clarity/structure. Export at the largest size you'll need.

**Know when to stop.** The standard failure is over-cooked: HDR-looking skies, haloed edges, orange skin, crushed shadows. Compare the edited version at 100% against the original. If texture has turned to plastic, step back two steps.

### 4. Cut it out, put it somewhere

Compositing is putting one image over another convincingly. In Photopea on a phone browser (use landscape, pinch-zoom, be patient — it's a desktop interface in a small window):

- **Select Subject** (Photopea has a magic-wand/quick-select; Canva and Adobe Express also have background removal, which is often faster and good enough).
- **Refine the edge** at hair and soft edges; hard cut-outs look pasted.
- **Put the subject on its own layer**, with the background layer below.
- **Match the light:** add a shadow under the object on a new layer (soft black brush at low opacity, or a copy of the subject flipped and blurred), and check that the light direction matches the background.
- **Match the colour:** add a subtle warmth/coolness layer so the subject and background share a colour temperature. A layer of the background's colour at 5–10% opacity, or a curves adjustment clipped to the subject, does it.
- **Blend modes you'll actually use:** *Multiply* to drop out white and add shadows/ink; *Screen* to add light, glows, and to drop out black; *Overlay/Soft light* for texture at low opacity.

The tell of a bad composite is mismatched shadows and edges. Fix those two and it reads as real at thumbnail size, which is where it will be seen.

### 5. Judging an image for design fitness

A beautiful photo is not necessarily a usable design asset. Ask:

- Does it have a **quiet area** for type?
- Does it survive **greyscale** — is there value separation between subject and background?
- Does it survive **thumbnail size** — is the subject recognisable at 100 px?
- Does it fit the **palette** without fighting it?
- Is there **space to crop** for the other formats you'll need?

If two of those fail, don't use the photo. Re-shoot or design without an image. A strong typographic layout beats a weak photo every time.

> **Try it (6 min):** photograph your object three ways — window light from the side with a white bounce, overhead room light, and with the phone torch on. Then compare the three raws side by side. You'll never use the torch again.

## Guided practice

1. **(15 min) The five-minute product shot.** Object on white paper near a window, white paper on the far side as a bounce. Shoot: square-on, from above, and a detail crop. Don't edit yet.
2. **(15 min) Edit one properly, in order.** Snapseed, steps 1–7. Save the intermediate as well as the final. Write the order you used in your log — this is your checklist from now on.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Because later steps depend on earlier ones. If you set contrast before white balance, correcting the cast afterwards shifts the contrast you carefully set. If you crop after adding local adjustments, your masks now sit in the wrong places. If you sharpen before resizing down for export, you lose the sharpening; if you sharpen after and then resize, you get artefacts. A classic wrong order: saturation before white balance — you end up with an overall cast baked into the colour.
2. A white surface on the shadow side bounces light back into the subject, filling the shadows so the phone's limited dynamic range doesn't have to choose between blown highlights and black shadows. It improves the *data captured*, so the edit has real detail to work with. A filter only redistributes the data you already have — it cannot recover what was never recorded.
3. Ranked: (1) ask for the original file (highest value, free); (2) use it at small size only (social, thumbnail) and design so the image is not the focal point; (3) upscale with care and accept softness, applying a little sharpening — acceptable for a small print, honest about the limits; (4) shoot a new photo yourself; (5) replace with a texture or illustration. Never silently print 800 px at A4 (that's ~96 dpi — visibly soft at reading distance).
4. **Multiply** darkens: whites become transparent, dark areas stay. Use it for shadows on a cut-out object, or to lay ink/texture over an image. **Screen** lightens: blacks become transparent, light areas show. Use it for glows, light leaks, smoke, or dropping a black-background logo art onto a photo.
5. **Shadows/light direction** that don't match between subject and background, and **edges** that are too hard or show a halo/fringe. A third tell worth naming: mismatched colour temperature or sharpness between the subject and the scene.
6. (a) Use a scrim — a dark or blurred band behind the type, which also becomes a design element; (b) crop in so the busy area falls outside the frame, or zoom into a quiet detail; (c) place the type off the image entirely, in a colour block beside it (a split layout: half photo, half solid colour). Also valid: reduce the photo's contrast in one area, or print the headline over the darkest region with reversed-out white text at a large size.

# Day 8 — Logo design: from brief to mark

**Phase:** Craft · **Week 2, day 8** · **Study slot:** Monday

## Why this matters

A logo is the smallest, most reproduced, most misunderstood piece of design work. Clients think it's the whole brand; beginners think it's a drawing; both miss the point. A logo's only job is **identification** — to be recognised, remembered, and reproduced anywhere. It doesn't sell, doesn't explain, and doesn't have to contain the business's story.

Today you do the real process: brief, words, fifty sketches, three concepts, one refinement, and a brutal test at small sizes. You are not allowed to skip to the "nice one" — the process is what makes the outcome good, and the process is what your portfolio case study will show.

## Lesson

### 1. The seven types of mark

| Type | What it is | Works when | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Wordmark** | The name set in distinctive type | The name is short, distinctive, and pronounceable | Needs a strong name; hard if the name is long |
| **Lettermark / monogram** | Initials | The name is long; you need a compact icon | Meaningless until the name is known |
| **Pictorial mark** | A recognisable object | The object is central to the business and not generic | Hard to make unique; literal |
| **Abstract mark** | A non-literal geometric form | You want a distinctive, ownable shape | Risk of meaning nothing; needs brand support |
| **Mascot** | A character | Family brands, food, sport, kids | Ages badly; hard to draw consistently |
| **Emblem** | Mark enclosed in a shape (badge) | Institutions, coffee, breweries, government | Loses detail when small; hard to use alone |
| **Combination** | Mark + wordmark | Most common, most flexible | Needs a defined lockup or it drifts |

For a phone-only beginner: **wordmark**, **lettermark** or **combination** is the realistic route. Choose a mark type *because it fits the name and the usage*, not because a pictorial mark looks more impressive in your head.

### 2. What a logo actually has to do

Four jobs, in order of importance:

1. **Identify** — distinguish this business from the one next door.
2. **Reproduce** — work at 16 px, on a sign, in one colour, embroidered, screen-printed, stamped on a receipt.
3. **Endure** — not be fashionable in a way that looks dated in two years.
4. **Signal** — carry some tone: playful, clinical, traditional, modern.

Notice what's missing: "tell the story of the business". Story lives in the brand, the identity system, the voice, the photography. A mark that tries to say "fast, reliable, affordable, and family-owned" ends up as a truck with a clock and a heart on it.

### 3. The process (do not shortcut this)

**Step 1 — Write the brief, in this order.** Business name, audience, three adjectives, what it must never look like, where it will appear, and the one competitor whose logo you must not resemble.

**Step 2 — Word association, five minutes.** Write 20 words the business suggests. Not concepts: words. Then mark the two or three you could actually draw. *This is where marks come from, not from opening Canva.*

**Step 3 — Fifty thumbnails, two per minute.** 50 small boxes on paper (or in Ibis Paint), each 15–20 seconds. No erasing, no judging. You will hate most of them; that's the point. Quantity produces the two or three ideas you'd never have reached by trying to be clever once. Do not start the app until all 50 exist.

**Step 4 — Shortlist three.** Pick by these criteria: recognisable at 16 px, distinct from the competitor, defensible in one sentence. Not "prettiest".

**Step 5 — Build one properly.** Draw geometric shapes in Canva (or Ibis Paint for a hand-drawn feel), set the wordmark in type, and then tune: optical size of dots and gaps, weight consistency between mark and letterforms, spacing between mark and name, the baseline relationship.

**Step 6 — Test brutally.** See section 5.

**Step 7 — Hand off.** Deliver PNG at 1000 px, a white/transparent version, a black version, and (when you get laptop access) SVG. Name files properly.

### 4. The visual tricks that make marks feel clever

- **Negative space** — a shape inside a shape (an arrow in the space between letters). Works only at large sizes; test it small.
- **Closure** — an incomplete form the eye completes. Great for movement and modernity.
- **Dual reading** — reads as a letter at one size and an object at another. Risky, but memorable.
- **Geometric construction** — build on circles and squares with consistent radii. This is what makes a mark feel "designed" rather than "assembled": shared geometry.
- **Monoline consistency** — every stroke the same weight. Mixed weights look amateur unless the contrast is deliberate and structural.

**Optical corrections you must make** (they are not optional, and they're what "professional" looks like):
- A circle must extend slightly beyond a square's edge to look the same size.
- Dots and small circles need a hair more size.
- Two shapes meeting at a point need a tiny gap or they read as overlapping.
- Text next to a mark often needs a small baseline nudge, not a mathematical centring.

### 5. The test sheet (make one, reuse it forever)

Put your mark through all of these, side by side on one page:

1. **16 px** — favicon size. Is it still distinguishable?
2. **Black on white**, one colour only.
3. **White on black** (reversed). Does it survive the inversion, or do counters fill in?
4. **On a busy photo** — does it need a scrim? (If yes, that's a brand rule, not a logo failure.)
5. **At 5 metres** — step back and look at the phone. Does it read as a shape?
6. **Next to the competitor's logo** — is it distinguishable at a glance?
7. **Squint test** — is there one clear silhouette?

If it fails 1, 2 or 3, redesign. Those three are non-negotiable; the mark will be used in those conditions constantly.

### 6. What is *not* your job

- **Not** to include the business's services as icons. That's a diagram, not a logo.
- **Not** to draw an animal in Canva's shape library and call it a lion.
- **Not** to make it photorealistic. If a mark needs a gradient and a shadow to work, it doesn't work.
- **Not** to please everyone. A logo that everyone likes is usually a logo that nobody remembers. Aim for *appropriate and distinctive*, then let the identity system do the charm.

> **Try it (5 min):** take your capstone business name and set it in four different typefaces at the same size. Screenshot. One will already feel like the brand. That's your starting point for the wordmark, and it's free.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Because logos are seen in conditions where explanation is impossible — a 16 px avatar, a receipt, a sign passed at 60 km/h. Their job is to *be recognised again*, not to explain on first sight. The monogram gives you something usable at small sizes; the short name gives you something readable on the sign.
4. For the client: a template logo is not exclusive (other businesses will have it), it isn't built for their reproduction conditions, and it comes with no file set, no clear space, no one-colour version, so every future use degrades. For you: you cannot charge for a template, cannot defend it in a conversation, and cannot show it in a portfolio as your work. Templates are a legitimate starting point; they are not a deliverable.
5. **Negative space**: the empty area inside or between forms reads as a shape (the arrow between the E and x). Risk: it often disappears at small sizes, so it must be tested at 16 px. **Closure**: the eye completes an incomplete shape, which feels dynamic and modern. Risk: if the gap is too big, the form becomes ambiguous and the mark stops being recognisable; if too small, it just looks like a printing error.

# Day 9 — Brand identity systems

**Phase:** Craft · **Week 2, day 9** · **Study slot:** Tuesday

## Why this matters

This is the day you stop being someone who makes pictures and become someone who makes **systems**. A client who buys a logo gets one file and comes back in three months with a poster that doesn't match it. Systems are also where the money and the professionalism live: an identity sheet is a deliverable you can charge for, and it's what separates a designer from a person with Canva. And if you're feeling discouraged today (Day 9 is when most people do), read the box at the end of the lesson.

## Lesson

### 1. Logo ≠ identity

| Logo | Identity |
| --- | --- |
| One mark | The whole system around the mark |
| Identifies | Makes everything recognisable |
| A file | A set of rules |
| Fixed | Applied, and therefore varied |

An identity answers: how does this brand look on a sign, a receipt, a uniform, a Facebook post, a delivery van, an invoice, a business card, a price list, a WhatsApp profile? If your answer is "the same logo in the corner", you have a logo, not an identity.

### 2. The components of a one-page brand sheet

You are producing **one A4 page** today. It must contain:

1. **The logo** in its primary lockup, large.
2. **Lockups:** horizontal, stacked, icon-only. Show which to use when.
3. **Clear space:** the protected area around the mark. Define it as a unit — "x = the height of the letter N; keep 1x clear on all sides". Show it with a dashed box.
4. **Minimum size:** the smallest the logo may be printed or shown (e.g. "20 mm wide in print, 32 px on screen"). Justify it by the test at that size.
5. **Colour system:** swatches with **names, hex codes and roles** (primary, secondary, accent, neutrals, plus the text/background pairing that passes contrast).
6. **Type system:** headline and body typefaces, the three-size scale, weights allowed, and one fallback per typeface (because the client won't have your font).
7. **Usage rules — the "don'ts":** stretch, recolour, rotate, add shadow, place on busy photos without a scrim, outline, or crowd it.
8. **One image/tone rule:** how photography should look (light, colour, subject), and three adjectives for voice with a "we never sound like" line.
9. **Contact / owner:** who owns the files and where they live.

That's a lot for one page, and that constraint is the exercise. If it doesn't fit, you're including things that aren't rules.

### 3. Clear space and minimum size, properly

**Clear space** exists because a logo crowded by other elements stops reading as a unit. Define it in relation to the logo itself, not in millimetres, so it scales: "1x on all sides, where x is the cap height of the wordmark". Show it once, visibly, with a dashed boundary.

**Minimum size** is discovered, not guessed: shrink your logo until it stops being legible, then note the size one step above that. Do it twice — once for print, once for screen. Different media, different limits.

### 4. Colour roles, not colour lists

A palette is not a list of five hex codes. It is a set of **roles with rules**:

| Role | Purpose | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | The brand's main colour | Used on the largest surfaces and the logo |
| Secondary | Supports the primary | Used for blocks, sections, secondary buttons |
| Accent | The spotlight | Max 10% of any layout; one per page |
| Neutrals | Backgrounds, text, rules | Must include a near-white and a near-black |
| Functional | Success / warning / error | Reserved — never decorative |

Add the **approved pairings**: "text on primary", "text on accent", "white logo on photo" — with contrast ratios noted. This is what makes a palette usable by someone who isn't a designer, and it's the difference between a brand sheet and a mood board.

### 5. Type systems and fallbacks

For each typeface, specify:

- **Where it's used** (headlines only / body only).
- **Weights allowed** (two, maximum three).
- **The scale** in actual numbers, with the medium named: "Print A4: headline 36 pt / deck 14 pt / body 10 pt" and "Screen: 32 px / 18 px / 15 px".
- **A fallback** — the closest widely available font, e.g. "Playfair Display → Georgia", "Montserrat → Arial". Without this, the first person who opens the file on a different device will break the brand.
- **What not to do** — never below X size, never all-caps for body, never the display face for paragraphs.

### 6. Applications prove the system

A brand sheet is a promise; applications are the proof. Test the system on three things you don't control: a **WhatsApp profile avatar** (square, small, on a colour), a **shop sign or banner** (large, far, one colour), and a **receipt or invoice** (tiny, monochrome, ugly by default). If the system survives those three, it's real.

> **Try it (4 min):** make a 200×200 px canvas and place your logo in it. Then view it in your gallery at thumbnail size. If you can't identify it in half a second, your icon lockup needs work — this is the single most-used version of your brand.

### 7. When it feels like it's not good enough

Day 9 is where the doubt lands: the logo looks simple, the palette looks plain, other people's work looks better. Two things are true. First, **simple is the goal** — the marks you admire are simple because simple survives. Second, you are looking at your own work at 400% zoom while everyone else will see it at 40 px, on a phone, for one second. Judge it at that size. If it holds there, it's working.

If you genuinely think the mark is weak, don't start over — do a **refinement pass**: harmonise stroke weights, fix the optical spacing, and simplify by one element. Ninety per cent of weak marks are over-complicated, not badly conceived.

## Answers and self-marking

1. With a logo only, they can paste it onto something and hope. With an identity, they can brief a printer, a sign-writer, an employee or a freelancer and get a consistent result: they know the colours by hex and role, the type by name and fallback, the clear space, the minimum size, and the rules for photos and voice. That's the difference between a file and a system — and it's why identity work is priced higher.
2. Because the rule has to scale. A logo used at 20 mm and the same logo on a 2 m banner need proportional protection; a fixed millimetre value would be absurdly large at one size and invisible at the other. Tying clear space to an internal measurement (cap height, icon height) means anyone can apply it at any size.
3. (a) They're printing in CMYK and the colour was chosen in RGB — saturated RGB blues and greens shift visibly; (b) they're printing a spot/one-colour job and approximated the colour, or there's no Pantone reference. 4. Because the client's computer, phone, printer and browser almost certainly do not have your font installed, and font substitution changes the layout: different widths break line breaks, different x-heights change perceived size. A documented fallback keeps the layout close and makes it clear what to expect. It also protects you from "the poster looks wrong" calls.
5. A palette is a set of colours. A colour system assigns **roles and rules** — dominant, support, accent, neutrals, functional — plus approved pairings with contrast ratios and prohibitions. A palette gives someone colours; a system tells them what to do with them.
6. Because a colour with no limit will be used as a background, then as a headline colour, then as a button, and within a month the brand is "the red one" in a way that fights every layout. A maximum turns a taste preference into an enforceable rule and preserves the accent's function: scarcity makes attention.

## Lesson

### 1. The six numbers of a print file

| Term | What it means | Set it to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trim size** | The final cut size | A4 = 210 × 297 mm, A5 = 148 × 210 mm, business card = 85 × 55 mm |
| **Bleed** | Artwork that extends past the trim so mis-cuts don't show white edges | 3 mm on all four sides |
| **Safe area** | Keep text and logos away from the cut | 5 mm inside the trim (10 mm for comfort) |
| **Resolution** | Detail at final size | 300 dpi for photos at 100% scale |
| **Colour mode** | How ink is described | CMYK for print; RGB for screen |
| **Marks** | Crop marks and registration | Included in a "PDF Print" export |

If you remember nothing else: **backgrounds must bleed, text must not.**

In Canva: turn on **File → Show print bleed** and **Show rulers and guides**. The bleed area appears as a hatched border — every background colour, photo or shape must extend into it. Then export **PDF Print**, which includes crop marks and bleed, rather than PNG, which does not.

### 2. Resolution, in plain arithmetic

Printed image quality depends on pixels *per inch at final size*. You can work it out:

```
required pixels = physical size in inches × 300
A4 full bleed (216 × 303 mm) at 300 dpi ≈ 2551 × 3579 px
A5 (154 × 216 mm) at 300 dpi          ≈ 1819 × 2551 px
2 m banner at 1:10 scale, 300 dpi      ≈ 2362 × 709 px  (i.e. 200 × 60 mm file)
```

For large formats, no one prints 2 metres at 300 dpi — it's viewed from metres away, so 100–150 dpi at full size is normal, and working at 1:10 scale at 300 dpi gives you that automatically. Practically: **flyers at 300 dpi, posters at 150 dpi, banners at 1:10 scale.**

If the client's photo is too small, don't silently upscale. Either use it small, ask for the original, or shoot a new one. Say which, in writing.

### 3. Colour: why the screen lies

- Screens emit light (RGB, additive). Paper reflects light off ink (CMYK, subtractive). They cannot match exactly.
- Saturated RGB blues, greens and neons are outside the CMYK gamut — they will print duller or shift.
- **Rich blacks:** pure black (C0 M0 Y0 K100) can look grey and thin on large areas. For big black areas, printers often use a rich black (e.g. C60 M40 Y40 K100) — ask your printer what they prefer.
- **Spot colour (Pantone)** is a pre-mixed ink. It's the only way to guarantee a brand colour, and it costs extra. Use it for brand-critical work at volume; skip it for a 100-piece flyer run.
- **Proofing:** for any run you're paying for, ask for a physical proof of one copy. It's cheap; a wrong run is not.

### 4. Paper: the cheapest quality upgrade

| Weight | Feels like | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 80–100 gsm | Photocopy paper, flimsy | Inserts, internal documents, cheap handouts |
| 130–170 gsm | Decent leaflet, holds a fold | Flyers, menus, leaflets (the sensible default) |
| 250–300 gsm | Card, substantial | Business cards, invitations, postcards, covers |
| 350 gsm+ | Board, premium | Luxury cards, packaging, covers |

Finish matters as much as weight: **matte/uncoated** feels warmer and takes writing, but colours look flatter; **gloss** makes photos pop but shows fingerprints and can look cheap if overused; **silk/satin** is the safe middle. If a client's budget allows one upgrade, it's paper weight, not more colours.

### 5. Designing for print on a phone, honestly

Do these and a phone file will pass at almost any print shop:

- Create the document at the **exact physical size** in Canva (A4, A5, banner at 1:10), never "resize later".
- Turn on bleed and guides **before** you place anything.
- Keep a **10 mm safe margin** for text instead of the minimum 5 mm — phone editing makes it easy to drift close to the edge.
- Check every photo's resolution by looking at its pixel dimensions, not by trusting the preview.
- View the design at **50% zoom** to see the whole page; phone zoom flatters everything.
- Export **PDF Print**, then reopen it and zoom to 100% to check for stray text and clipping.

> **Try it (5 min):** make an A4 document, set a background colour, turn on bleed, and see whether the colour actually fills the hatched area. Nine times out of ten it doesn't on the first attempt. Fix it now, not at the printer's counter.

### 6. Adapting, not scaling

The same message in three formats is three designs that share a system, not one design shrunk:

- **A4 poster (portrait):** vertical hierarchy, big type, image occupies the top or the background.
- **Square social (1:1):** tighter crop, larger type relative to the frame, fewer words.
- **Story (9:16):** one idea, huge type, text inside the central band, everything else pushed out.

Rebuild the layout on a new canvas using the same grid logic, palette and type scale. If you merely scale, the type becomes too small and the margins become wrong.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Because of what the cut does. The guillotine is not perfectly accurate, so a background that ends exactly at the trim can leave a 1 mm white sliver — extending it 3 mm past the trim guarantees full coverage after cutting. Text, by contrast, must stay *inside* the safe area because anything near the edge risks being sliced through or feeling cramped. Backgrounds bleed; text retreats.
2. 1200 ÷ 300 = 4 inches ≈ 102 mm; 800 ÷ 300 = 2.7 inches ≈ 68 mm. So at 300 dpi it covers only about 102 × 68 mm — less than a quarter of A4. Options: request the original file (best); use it at a small size within the layout rather than full bleed; photograph a replacement; or, as a last resort, upscale and accept softness, but do not print it full-bleed at A4 and expect quality. State the arithmetic to the client — it ends the argument politely.
3. CMYK builds colour from four inks in tiny dots; spot colour is a single pre-mixed ink printed from its own plate. Spot guarantees an exact brand colour (e.g. a logo's red) and can include metallics or fluorescents that CMYK cannot reproduce. It's worth the cost when a brand colour is critical and the run is large enough to absorb setup cost, or when the colour is simply outside CMYK's range. For small runs, CMYK plus a proof is usually the right call.
4. (a) It's pure K100 on a large area, which reads thin and grey next to rich photographic blacks; printers often want a rich black (mixed CMYK) for large solids. (b) It's being viewed against a bright background, or the paper is uncoated and absorbing ink, flattening the density. Ask the printer their preferred rich-black build for the stock — they will have one.
5. Because paper is the thing the customer physically touches, and it signals quality before any design decision is noticed. A well-designed flyer on 170 gsm uncoated feels considered; the same design on 80 gsm photocopy paper feels like a handout. A fourth colour, by contrast, is often used to add noise, not clarity.
6. Because a PDF embeds fonts, preserves vector shapes and type as crisp edges rather than pixels, carries the correct physical dimensions and bleed box, and includes crop marks. A PNG is a fixed pixel grid: it can't tell the printer the trim size, it can't scale, and small text becomes soft at print resolution.

## Why this matters

Up to now you've made single pieces and responded to briefs. ## Lesson

### 1. Concept before content

A **concept** is a decision about how to say the thing, not what the thing is. "Fresh bread" is a fact. "Morning light on a just-broken loaf, no props, no people" is a concept — it decides light, subject, framing and mood at once.

Write it as two sentences:

> **Single-minded proposition:** *(the one thing the audience must feel or believe)*
> Students in Kampala deserve a study space that feels like it takes them seriously.
>
> **Concept statement:** *(how the images will say it)*
> Quiet, warm, low-angle photographs of empty desks in late-afternoon light, with one object per frame, no people, no clutter. Everything shot at eye level as if you're sitting down.

If your concept could describe any brand in the category, it isn't a concept yet.

### 2. The three-image structure

Most small campaigns are built from this trio, and it's the easiest to execute alone:

| Shot | Job | Typical frame |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Establishing** | Sets place and mood | Wider, environment visible, room to breathe, space for type |
| **Detail** | Proves quality and texture | Close crop, shallow depth, one object |
| **Human / moment** | Creates feeling and scale | Hands, a person, an action — not necessarily a face |

Three images, one rule set, and you have a campaign. Five images with five different moods is a stock library.

### 3. The rules that make a set a family

Decide these **before** you shoot, and write them down. Then obey them in every frame:

1. **Light direction** — always from the same side (e.g. window at 45° left). This alone creates family resemblance.
2. **Colour** — one palette, one temperature. If frame one is warm, frame three cannot be cool.
3. **Framing** — same height, same distance logic, same amount of headroom. Eye-level for all three, or from above for all three.
4. **Density** — how much is in the frame. Minimal, medium, or busy — choose one.
5. **Props** — a maximum of two, repeated. Repetition is what makes them a system.
6. **Posture/gesture** — if there are people, the same kind of gesture (hands working, not posing).
7. **Crop and ratio** — one ratio, usually 4:5 or 1:1 for social, consistent across the set.

### 4. Mood boards that are actually useful

A mood board is not a wish list of beautiful images. It's a **specification** with four rows:

| Row | What to collect | What it decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light | 3 images with similar light quality | Direction, hardness, time of day |
| Colour | 3 images, or 5 swatches | Palette and temperature |
| Texture / material | 3 close-ups | Paper, fabric, surface, grain |
| Framing / composition | 3 images | Height, crop, negative space |

Then add a fifth row: **"not this"** — three images that show what you must avoid. The "not this" row prevents more revisions than anything else on the board, because it makes the unspoken explicit.

### 5. Directing on a phone, alone

- **You are the model.** Shoot with a 10-second timer, prop the phone on books, and shoot 30 frames to get one. That's normal and it's free.
- **Direct a friend with specifics, not adjectives.** Not "look natural" — "hold the cup with both hands and look at the window, not the camera".
- **Shoot in bursts**, then stop. Choose two frames, edit both, keep one. Endless shooting is procrastination with a camera.
- **Move the subject, not the light.** Put the object near the window; don't chase the sun around the room.
- **Kill the clutter.** The single biggest upgrade to amateur photography is removing objects from the frame.

### 6. Shooting to a grid, then sequencing

Plan the **set as a page** before you shoot: three frames that will sit together in a carousel or a grid. If all three have the subject dead centre, the set will look monotonous; if all three are wide, it will look empty. Vary the distance (wide / close / medium) while keeping light, palette and framing angle constant. That's how a set feels composed without feeling repeated.

### 7. Critiquing a campaign

Don't ask "do I like it". Ask:

1. What is the single-minded proposition? Can I state it after seeing the set?
2. Do the three images agree on light, palette and framing — or is one an orphan?
3. Does each image have a distinct job, or is it a repeat?
4. Would the set survive the client's worst use case — cropped to a square, at thumbnail size, in a feed of other brands?
5. What's the weakest frame, and what would I reshoot?

> **Try it (5 min):** write your three rules — light, colour, framing — in one line each, before you pick up the phone. If you can't write them, you'll be making three separate photos.

## Guided practice

1. **(10 min) Proposition and concept.** Two sentences for your capstone brand, using the format above. Read it back: could it describe a competitor? If yes, sharpen it.
2. **(10 min) Mood board with rows.** Five rows (light, colour, texture, framing, not-this), three images each, in Canva. Label each row.

5. **(15 min) Edit to match.** Edit all three *together*, switching between them, rather than finishing one at a time. Match exposure, temperature and contrast across the set. This is the single most important editing habit in art direction.
6. **(10 min) Sequence and check.** Put the three side by side at phone size. Any image that looks like a different brand gets re-edited or dropped.

## Answers and self-marking

1. A mood board specifies rules: it has categories (light, colour, texture, framing), and it exists to answer decisions before you shoot. A reference pile is a collection of things you like, with no categories and no conclusions. The test: can someone else shoot from your board without asking you a question? If yes, it's a mood board.
2. Because a set is read as one object. If one frame is lit from the left and another from the right, the eye reads two different places and times, and the set loses its authority — it looks assembled rather than directed. Individual beauty doesn't survive being placed next to something that contradicts it.
3. Establishing sets place and mood and gives the viewer context; detail proves texture and quality up close; human/moment gives scale, emotion and relatability. The detail shot is usually the most persuasive because trust in physical products is built on evidence of quality — the grain of the fabric, the crispness of the fold — which a wide shot can't show.
4. (a) Different light direction or quality; (b) different colour temperature or palette; (c) different framing height or distance logic; (d) different editing (one more contrasty or more saturated than the others). Also valid: mismatched props, or inconsistent crops/ratios.
5. In this order: (1) who is it for and where will they see it; (2) what should they think or do; (3) which brands or images feel right, and which feel wrong; (4) what must never appear (logo misuse, colours, types of people). "Professional" is the outcome; those four are the inputs. Get three "not this" references before you design anything.
6. Because consistency is a relationship between images, and you cannot see a relationship one image at a time. Editing together forces you to match exposure, temperature and contrast in context, and it catches the frame that has drifted while you weren't looking. It also prevents you from over-editing one image that then makes the others look flat.

## Lesson

### 1. Templates are a system, not a shortcut

A template is the brand rules made executable. Build it properly once and the next twenty posts take five minutes each. Build it badly and every post needs fixing.

What to **lock** (so it can't drift): logo position and size, margins, the type scale, the palette, the corner radius of shapes, the position of the "swipe" indicator.

What to **leave open**: image, headline, one accent colour, body copy length.

In Canva: build a page, then use **Duplicate page** to create variants (image-led, quote-led, offer-led, list-led), and save them as a **template set** in your GD Course folder. Never edit a finished post to make the next one — start from the template.

### 2. The five-post carousel

Carousels work because each swipe is a small commitment. Their structure is a story, not a slide deck:

| Post | Job | Design rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Hook | Stop the scroll and promise something | Biggest type of the set; one sentence; a reason to swipe |
| 2 — Tension | State the problem or the surprising fact | Fewer words than you think; one image or none |
| 3 — Substance | Deliver the value, step or proof | Numbered, scannable, consistent treatment |
| 4 — Proof | Evidence: result, testimonial, before/after, detail | The image does the work; caption is short |
| 5 — Action | Tell them exactly what to do | One action, one way to do it, brand mark |

Rules that make the difference: same margins on every slide so the set reads as one object; a visible **swipe cue** on slides 1–4; one idea per slide; and no slide that only repeats the previous one. If slide 2 can be deleted without loss, delete it.

### 3. Batching: the only way to survive a retainer

A one-week batch in 45 minutes:

1. **5 min** — write the week's five headlines in a note. Not in the design app. Copy first.
2. **5 min** — choose one image per post from your existing library (Day 11's shoot gives you a month).
3. **25 min** — duplicate the templates, paste, adjust one accent element each. Do not redesign.
4. **10 min** — export all, name by convention (`capstone-2026-w1-post1.png`), file them.

The discipline is **decide then execute**. Beginners alternate between deciding and decorating, which is why one post takes 40 minutes.

### 4. Motion: three principles and you're done

You are not learning After Effects today. You are learning to make a post that moves with intent.

1. **Ease, don't snap.** Things accelerate and decelerate. In CapCut, use ease-in/ease-out (or "curve" presets) rather than linear. A linear move reads as cheap; an eased one reads as designed.
2. **One movement at a time, in hierarchy order.** The headline arrives first, then the image, then the call to action. If everything moves at once, nothing is emphasised. Stagger entrances by 0.3–0.5 s.
3. **Hold before and after.** A 1-second hold at the start and a 1.5-second hold at the end makes a 6-second animation feel intentional and gives the eye time to read. Never have the last frame change instantly before the loop.

Duration guide: **5–8 seconds** for a feed animation; 15 seconds is a video, not a post. Loop if it's for stories — end on the same frame you started.

### 5. Exporting without breaking things

| Destination | Ratio | Format | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feed post (static) | 4:5 or 1:1 | PNG | Text inside the central 80% |
| Carousel | 4:5 or 1:1 | PNG per slide, numbered | Consistent margins across slides |
| Story / Reel | 9:16 | MP4 or PNG | Keep text out of top 10% and bottom 15% |
| Animated post | 4:5 or 1:1 | MP4, high quality | Loop point; no black frames at the start |
| TikTok | 9:16 | MP4 | Assume sound off; text must carry meaning |

Always watch your export **on the phone, at actual size, in the app** before calling it done. Compression, UI overlays and autoplay all change what the design looks like.

### 6. Copy is design

Half of what makes social design work is the words being the right length. Write headlines to fit the layout — 6 to 9 words for a hook, not 20. If a headline won't fit your type scale, the headline is wrong, not the type size. Cut words until the layout breathes; that's a design act performed with a pen.

> **Try it (5 min):** open your template. Type a 20-word headline. Watch it break. Now cut it to 7 words and set it at the same size. The second version is the one that gets read — and it took 30 seconds to find.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Because the expensive part is not doing the work, it's **switching contexts and re-deciding**. Batching removes the decisions: the layout is fixed, the palette is fixed, the type scale is fixed, so each post becomes "choose image, write headline, adjust one accent". It also improves consistency, because all the posts are made under the same mental model rather than across seven different moods and days.
2. **Lock**: margins, logo position and size, type scale and sizes, palette, corner radii, swipe indicator, safe areas. **Open**: image, headline and body copy, one accent colour, and the choice of variant. Anything that carries the brand identity is locked; anything that carries the specific message is open.
3. A single post has to land one idea in one glance. A carousel has to earn a sequence of glances, so it needs a narrative structure — hook, tension, substance, proof, action — where each slide creates a reason to see the next. Structurally that's closer to a short article than to a poster, so the typography also shifts from display-led to reading-led.
4. "Ease" means the movement starts slow, speeds up in the middle, and slows again at the end — like a door opening under its own weight. Linear movement runs at exactly the same speed from start to finish, which nothing physical does, so it reads as machine-generated. It also draws attention to the mechanism instead of the content, which is the opposite of what good motion does.
5. (a) Text inside the platform's UI overlay (top 10% / bottom 15% for stories); (b) colour shift or banding from compression, especially on subtle gradients and saturated reds; (c) autoplay with sound off, so any message carried by audio is lost. Also valid: the first frame being a black or blank frame, so the post looks broken in a feed thumbnail.
6. Options: (1) raise the price and explain what the extra work costs to produce, offering fewer, better posts at the same budget; (2) deliver five designed posts plus ten template-based variations the client fills in themselves — same deliverable count, honest about the difference in craft. What you must not do is silently produce fifteen rushed posts, because the inconsistent output will be attributed to your skill, not to the budget.

# Day 13 — UX/UI: designing screens that make sense

**Phase:** Advanced · **Week 3, day 13** · **Study slot:** Monday
**Time:** 2 h 30 m · **Tools today:** Figma (browser + mobile app), Canva
**You need:** your capstone brand assets, and one app you use every day

## Why this matters

The highest-paid design work available to a self-taught designer right now is product work — apps, websites, booking flows, dashboards. And the barrier is lower than people think: the core skill is not software, it's **thinking in flows, states and constraints** rather than in single images.

Today you'll design one screen properly: a wireframe first, then a high-fidelity version, with real content, correct touch targets, empty and error states, and a prototype someone can tap. That's enough to get a first paid job fixing someone's booking screen. It's also the day that makes all your poster skills suddenly seem small — screens have to *work*, not just look right.

- Build a low-fidelity wireframe before any colour or type.
- Design a screen with a spacing system, a type scale and correct touch targets.
- Cover the states most beginners forget: empty, loading, error, success, long-content.

## Lesson

### 1. UX and UI are different jobs

**UX** is the structure: what the user is trying to do, in what order, with what information, and what happens when things go wrong. **UI** is the surface: type, colour, spacing, components, iconography.

A beautiful screen that hides the "confirm" button behind a scroll is a UI success and a UX failure. Most local "app design" work is UI work sitting on top of unexamined UX — which is why it looks modern and still frustrates people. Learn to ask the UX questions first:

1. What is the one thing the user came here to do?
2. What do they need to know *at this moment* to do it?
3. What happens if they make a mistake?
4. What if there's no data yet? No connection? Too much data?

### 2. Flows before screens

Draw the flow as boxes and arrows — on paper, in your notes app, anywhere:

```
Entry (notification) → Home → [Service list] → Choose time → Confirm → Success
                                     ↓
                              Empty state (no slots)
                                     ↓
                              Alternative times / call us
```

Every decision point needs both outcomes drawn. Every failure needs a route out. A flow with no dead-end handling is a flow that confuses real users. Five minutes of boxes saves an hour of redesigning a screen that can't hold the content.

### 3. Wireframes: grey boxes, no opinions

A wireframe is grey rectangles, placeholder text, and real structure. Its purpose is to settle *layout and priority* before aesthetics can distract anyone — including you.

Rules:
- **Grey only.** No brand colour. Colour makes people discuss taste instead of structure.
- **Real content lengths.** Use the actual longest service name, the real price format, the real number of items. Fake short text hides the problems.
- **Label the components**, not the copy: "primary button", "list item", "price". You're designing a system.
- **One screen at a time**, at 390 × 844 px (a common phone frame). Design mobile-first; a desktop layout is a re-arrangement, not a starting point.

### 4. The four numbers of a usable screen

1. **Touch target:** minimum 44 × 44 px (Apple) / 48 × 48 dp (Android). If it's tappable, it's at least that big — including icon buttons and close crosses.
2. **Spacing system:** pick a base unit (8 px) and use multiples: 8, 16, 24, 32, 48. No arbitrary 13 px gaps. Consistency in spacing is what makes a screen feel "designed" more than any colour choice.
3. **Type scale for screens:** e.g. 28/22/17/15/13 px with line heights 1.2/1.3/1.4/1.5/1.4. Body never below 15 px on mobile; supporting text never below 13 px.
4. **Safe areas:** 16–20 px side margins on a 390 px screen; respect the notch and the home indicator; keep primary actions within thumb reach (the bottom third for one-handed use).

### 5. States: where amateur screens collapse

Design these five for any interactive element, and your screen will look more professional than 90% of dribbble shots:

| State | What it is | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Default** | The normal state | The one everyone designs |
| **Empty** | No data yet (no bookings, no results) | The first thing a new user sees; usually missing |
| **Loading** | Waiting, with feedback | Without it, people tap again and double-book |
| **Error** | Something failed, and what to do next | Should say what happened and offer the next action |
| **Success** | Confirmation | The moment trust is built |

Also design for: long text (a service name twice as long as expected), no connection, and a returning user (what's different).

### 6. Components, not screens

A screen is made of a small set of components: header, card, list item, button (primary/secondary/disabled), input, chip, tab bar, modal, empty-state block. Design each one properly *once* — with all its states — then assemble screens from them. This is what makes a design look like a product rather than a picture, and it's what a developer needs from you.

In Figma, use **Components** and **Auto layout** if you can; on a phone browser it's fiddly but workable if you keep the screen small and zoom in. Canva is a legitimate fallback for a single hi-fi screen — just build the components as separate grouped elements so they stay consistent.

### 7. Accessibility is not optional

- Contrast 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text and icons.
- Never convey meaning by colour alone (a red dot for "closed" needs a word or icon too).
- Labels above inputs, not placeholder-only — placeholders vanish as soon as someone types.
- Tap targets at least 44 px, with 8 px between adjacent targets.
- Support larger text: if your layout breaks when the font grows 30%, it's too tight.

> **Try it (5 min):** open your most-used app and find one screen where you have to think before tapping. Write down what the screen should have told you. That sentence is the beginning of a UX career.

## Guided practice

1. **(10 min) Flow on paper.** One user goal, five or six boxes, every decision with two arrows, every failure with a route out. Photograph it.
2. **(10 min) Wireframe.** 390 × 844 grey boxes in Figma (or Canva). Real content lengths, labelled components, nothing decorative. This should look boring; that's correct.
3. **(10 min) Spacing system.** Define your base unit and list the values you'll use. Set the side margins. Then check every element sits on the grid.
4. **(15 min) Hi-fi screen.** Apply your brand: colour, type scale, one accent, one image. Keep the structure from the wireframe — if the layout changed, ask why.
5. **(15 min) States.** Design the empty state, the loading state, and one error state for the same screen. Group them as a set.
6. **(10 min) Audit.** Check touch targets, contrast, and the longest text. Fix and note what you changed.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Example: a checkout screen with a gorgeous gradient, elegant type, and a "Place order" button that sits below the fold, so users scroll past it. UI: the surface is well crafted. UX: the single most important action isn't visible at the moment of decision. One sentence: UI is how it looks, UX is whether the person can accomplish what they came for.
2. Because colour and type invite aesthetic opinions before structure is agreed, and they hide layout problems (a busy background can disguise weak grouping). Grey boxes force the conversation onto hierarchy, grouping and flow — the decisions that are expensive to change later. Style is cheap to apply afterwards; structure is not.
3. The physical area a finger must hit to activate something. Minimum 44 × 44 px on iOS, 48 × 48 dp on Android. The number exists because a fingertip's contact patch is roughly 9–10 mm, and a smaller target produces mis-taps — especially for people with larger fingers, tremor, or using the phone one-handed while walking.
4. Default, empty, loading, error, success. The empty state matters most because every new user meets it first: with no bookings, no results, no history, the screen is at its least informative — and that's the moment a person decides whether the product is any good. A blank screen with no explanation reads as broken.
5. Because consistency in spacing is what the eye reads as quality, and because an arbitrary value creates near-misses (13 px next to 16 px looks wrong but is hard to name). A base unit plus multiples produces rhythm, makes handoff to a developer unambiguous, and speeds up decisions — you choose from a small set instead of inventing a number each time.
6. Before colour: what is the user trying to do on this screen, and what isn't working now? Then: what content must be visible at the moment of decision, what happens on error, what the empty state says, and what the primary action is. "Nicer" is usually a symptom of unclear hierarchy or too many competing elements, not a palette problem.

## Why this matters

## Lesson

### 1. Turn the request into a brief

A client's first message is never a brief. It's a wish: *"I need a poster for my business, something nice, by Friday."* Your job is to convert it into decisions — in writing, before you design.

Ask these seven questions, in this order:

1. Who is this for, and where will they see it? (audience + placement)
2. What should they do after seeing it? (objective)
3. What must appear, exactly? (mandatories: name, number, prices, logos, legal text)
4. What's the deadline, and is anything printing? (schedule + production)
5. Who approves, and how many people? (decision path)
6. What do you already have — logo, photos, colours, fonts? Are they usable?
7. What's your budget range?

Then send a **one-paragraph confirmation**: "Here's what I understand… Here's what I'll deliver… Here's what I need from you… Here's when." Two minutes to write, and it prevents most disasters. When a client later asks for something outside it, you're not arguing about memory — you're pointing at a document.

### 2. Pricing: a method, not a feeling

Never price by "what feels fair". Price by **scope × rate**, then adjust for risk.

```
Step 1  Estimate time honestly, in half-days:
        brief + research + sketches ....... 1 day
        concept development ............... 1 day
        refinement ........................ 1 day
        adaptation (print + social) ....... 0.5 day
        presentation + revisions .......... 0.5 day
        TOTAL .............................. 4 days

Step 2  Apply your day rate:
        4 days x [your day rate] = project fee

Step 3  Add a risk buffer of 10-20% for
        vague briefs, many approvers, or new clients.

Step 4  Add third-party costs separately (printing, fonts, stock photos).

Step 5  Take a deposit: 50% before work starts is normal and fair.
        Small jobs: 100% upfront. Long jobs: milestone payments.
```

**Setting your day rate from zero:** take the monthly income you need, divide by the number of days you can realistically bill in a month (12–16, not 22 — you need time for admin, marketing and learning), then add tax and expenses. If you need 1,500,000 a month in your currency and can bill 12 days, your day rate is at least 125,000 before tax and expenses. Round up, not down.

*The numbers above are illustrative. Check your local market by asking three designers what they charge, and price against the value of the work rather than against the cheapest competitor.*

**Four pricing models, and when they fit:**

| Model | Fits | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hourly** | Unclear scope, ongoing support, consulting | Punishes you for getting faster |
| **Fixed project** | Clear scope, defined deliverables | You absorb scope creep — hence the brief |
| **Retainer** | Monthly content, ongoing brand management | Must define volume and revision limits or it becomes a subscription to unlimited work |
| **Value-based** | High-impact work (a rebrand that sells more) | Needs a client who sees the value; hardest to sell locally but the most profitable |

### 3. Presenting: three concepts, one recommendation

The presentation is where projects are won or lost. Structure it in four sections:

1. **The brief back to them** — audience, objective, one message. This proves you listened and sets the criteria you'll be judged against.
2. **Concept 1 — the recommendation.** Say why it fits the brief. Do not hedge.
3. **Concepts 2 and 3 — the alternatives.** Different strategic directions, not different colours. "This one is bolder for a younger audience; this one is quieter and more premium."
4. **Next steps** — what you need, and when.

**Rules that prevent disaster:**

- Never show work you don't want chosen. Clients pick the weakest option surprisingly often when it's the safest.
- Recommend one, out loud: "If it were my business, I'd choose concept one, because…". Clients want a designer with judgement.
- Show the design **in context** (a mockup on a wall, a phone, a sign). Context makes abstract layouts legible.
- Don't present more than three. More options create paralysis, not choice.

### 4. Feedback: the three classic moves

**Move 1: "I don't like it."** Ask a diagnostic question, not a defensive one: "Which part — the colour, the type, or the overall feel?" Then: "What should a customer feel when they see it?" You are converting taste into criteria. If they still can't articulate it, show the same design in two variants of the element in question, and let them choose between two good options rather than between good and bad.

**Move 2: "Can you just try a few small changes?"** Count them out loud, politely: "That's four changes — two are within the revision round, two are new work. I can do the first two now and quote the others." Never do unlimited free iterations; it teaches the client that changes are free.

**Move 3: "My cousin / my wife / our chairman thinks it should be blue."** Ask who the approver is, and ask to have that person in the next review. Design by committee through intermediaries is the single biggest cause of projects that never end. If multiple people must approve, ask for **one** consolidated set of comments.

### 5. Saying no, and raising your price

- **"That's outside the scope"** — not "no". Offer it as a change order with a price and a new date.
- **"That's not a job I can do well"** — honest specialisation builds trust. Refer it to someone who can, and keep the relationship.
- **Red flags worth declining:** no deposit, "we'll pay after it works", unlimited approvers, a deadline that requires you to skip the brief, or a demand for free spec work ("design three options and we'll pick one and pay").
- **Raising prices:** do it for new clients first, give existing ones a month's notice, and justify it with what's changed (experience, demand, expanded scope) rather than with an apology.

### 6. Handover: end cleanly

Deliver a folder containing:

- Logo files: full colour, black, white/reversed, PNG at 1000 px, and vector where possible, plus icon-only.
- The brand sheet PDF.
- Print-ready PDFs, and source files if agreed — many designers keep source files and hand over exports. Say which in the quote.
- A one-page "how to use this" note: fonts and fallbacks, colour codes, minimum sizes, do/don't list.
- An invoice with terms, and written confirmation that the licence is granted on payment.

Then ask for two things: a testimonial, and permission to show the work in your portfolio. Both are worth more than the last revision.

> **Try it (5 min):** write your rate card — day rate, half-day rate, deposit policy, revision policy (how many rounds, what counts as a round) and what's excluded. One screen in your notes. This is the document that stops you improvising under pressure.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Because memory is unreliable under pressure, and the client's memory will be reshaped by what they now want. A written brief fixes the audience, objective, deliverables, mandatories and deadline at a moment when everyone agrees, and gives you a neutral object to point at: "This is what we agreed — this new request is a change, so let's price it." It also protects the client: it's the document that stops you from drifting.
2. 3 days × your day rate, plus 15% of that as a buffer; the deposit is 50% of the total, paid before work begins, with the balance due on delivery or approval. If your day rate is 150,000: 3 × 150,000 = 450,000; +15% = 517,500; deposit = 258,750; balance = 258,750. Third-party costs (printing, fonts, stock) are separate and usually paid upfront.
3. Because three is enough to show a range of strategic directions while keeping the decision simple. Five creates a comparison exercise and invites mixing elements across concepts ("I like the icon from three and the font from five"), which produces something worse than any of them. You recommend one because clients are buying your judgement, and a designer who won't commit is easier to overrule.
4. Ask for **one** consolidated set of comments, from one nominated approver, in one document — and explain why: parallel feedback channels produce contradictions no design can satisfy. If the director must be involved, get them into the presentation, or send the deck with a comment deadline. Then respond in writing, separating "included", "new work, quoted" and "won't work, and here's why".
5. A revision is an adjustment within the agreed direction: different colour, different spacing, different photo, a typo. New work is a change of direction or scope: a new concept, a new format, extra pages, a new deliverable. It must be in the quote because "revisions included" without a definition means unlimited work in the client's mind — and the argument always happens at the worst moment, when they're waiting and you're tired.
6. Because it filters out clients who were never going to pay, funds the work (materials, print, your time), and creates commitment: a client who has paid behaves differently — engages with the brief, gives feedback on time, takes the deadline seriously. It's also industry standard, so refusing a deposit marks a client as a risk.

## Lesson

### 1. What a portfolio actually is

It is not a gallery of everything you've made. It is an argument, made to one specific kind of client, that you can solve their problem. Three projects that are clearly explained beat thirty images with no context, every time.

Choose your three:

1. **The capstone** — your deepest project (identity + applications + print + social).
2. **The one that shows range** — the most different thing you made (the campaign, the leaflet, the app screen).
3. **The one that shows judgement** — the hardest problem, the best writing, the piece where you said no to something. If you did the Day 14 client reply, that document belongs here.

If a project doesn't help that argument, leave it out. A weak fourth project lowers the average of the first three.

### 2. The case study structure

One page per project, five sections, 300–500 words total. Every section earns its place:

| Section | What goes in it | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The problem** | Who the client is, who their audience is, and the specific difficulty. No adjectives, no "passionate about". | 2–3 sentences |
| **The constraints** | Budget, format, deadline, print method, one colour, no photos — whatever made it hard | 2–4 bullets |
| **The thinking** | The concept and the *decision* you made: what you chose and what you rejected, and why | 3–5 sentences |
| **The process** | 3–5 images: thumbnails, wireframe, grid, the rejected concept, the test sheet | 3–5 images |
| **The outcome** | What exists now, what it does, what the client said. If you have no numbers, say what changed qualitatively — never invent statistics. | 2–3 sentences |

**Writing rules:** no jargon ("synergy", "disruptive"), no "I'm passionate", no list of software. Write like a person explaining a decision to another person. One strong sentence about a decision is worth a paragraph about your process.

Example of the difference:

- *Weak:* "I created a modern, vibrant identity that reflects the brand's values and target audience."
- *Strong:* "The shop opens at 6am for commuters, so the logo had to read from across a road in low light. I chose a heavy wordmark over an icon because an icon would have needed a caption to be understood."

The second one is hireable.

### 3. Process shots: show the thinking, not the mess

Use 3–5 images per project, chosen to prove a point:

- **The 50 thumbnails**, cropped to a grid — proves you generate before you settle.
- **The wireframe or grid** — proves you structure before you decorate.
- **A rejected concept** — proves judgement; label it "rejected because…".
- **The test sheet** (16 px, one colour, reversed) — proves you understand reproduction.
- **The final in context** (a mockup on a wall, a phone, a sign) — proves it works in the world.

Do not show every screenshot you took. Curation is the skill being demonstrated.

### 4. The portfolio page

You need **one page** that survives a phone. Use Canva's Website feature (free, publishes to a link), Notion, or a Behance profile. Content order:

1. **Who you are and what you do**, one sentence. "I design brand identities and social content for small businesses in Kampala." Not "creative designer passionate about design".
2. **Three projects**, each a card with one strong image and a title.
3. **One expanded case study** (the capstone) on its own page.
4. **What you offer** — three services, with a starting price or "from…" line. Yes, put prices. It filters enquiries and signals professionalism.
5. **One clear contact action** — WhatsApp link, email, or a form. One, not four.
6. **A testimonial**, if you have one. If not, ask for one today; a friendly client will write it.

Design it with the skills you just learned: three-level hierarchy, two typefaces at most, one accent colour, real margins, and nothing that requires pinching to read. Test on your own phone, in daylight, at arm's length.

### 5. Honesty about your level

You are fifteen days in. You are not a senior designer, and pretending otherwise loses work you could have won. Position yourself accurately:

- "I design brand identities and social content for small businesses" — true, and exactly what most local businesses need.
- Don't claim photography, motion graphics, packaging or web development experience you don't have.
- Do show the process — clients can see competence in a wireframe even if they can't judge a gradient.
- Say what you're learning next. Curiosity reads as professional; overclaiming reads as a risk.

The best first clients are the ones whose work matches what you can genuinely do well right now: a logo and a brand sheet, a flyer that prints correctly, a month of consistent social posts, a clean menu.

Say what was real (the brief, the constraints, the printing) and what wasn't (the client). If you photographed a real shop with permission, say so. Then do two small real jobs in the next two weeks — a flyer for a friend's business, a logo for a church group — and replace the fictional work as fast as you can. Honesty plus momentum beats a fake client list, which is easily discovered.

## Research for tomorrow

You've finished the course. The research doesn't stop — it just becomes self-directed:

- **Figma, properly.** One hour on a borrowed laptop. Auto layout, components, styles. It's the highest-value next skill for paid work.
- **Print production, in person.** Ask a print shop to walk you through a job: file setup, proofing, paper, finishing. Then design something for actual press with their guidance.
- **A specialisation.** Pick one to go deep on: brand identity, social content systems, UI/UX, packaging, or motion. Follow five practitioners in that field and study their work weekly.
- **Business skills.** Read one book on freelancing (pricing, contracts, client management). Write your own simple contract.
- **A local design community.** Find one — a WhatsApp group, a studio that mentors, a meetup. Most paid work comes through people who have seen your work and trust you.
- **Teach one thing.** Explain visual hierarchy to someone in ten minutes. Teaching exposes the gaps in your understanding faster than another tutorial.

## Short on time

Do exercises 2, 4, 5 and the bakery reply (about 60 min). A published page with one case study beats a perfect page that never goes live. Publish today; refine on Saturday.

## Finish line

Fifteen days ago you had a phone. Now you have:

- A vocabulary that lets you diagnose design problems instead of guessing.
- A process: brief, research, thumbnail, build, test, present, hand over.
- A capstone identity with applications, print work, a campaign and a screen.
- The business documents: a rate card, a quote, a revision policy, a profile.
- A published case study and a portfolio page.
- A plan for the next ninety days.

That is a foundation, not a finished designer. The difference between people who get good and people who stay at day fifteen is simply whether they keep finishing work for real people. Go and find your first client this week — a small one, a cheap one, a real one.

**Come back to Day 3, Day 6 and Day 11 every month.** Composition, typesetting and art direction are the three skills that keep improving for a decade, and each pass will teach you something you couldn't see today.

## 2. Client questions to ask before quoting

1. Who is this for, and where will they see it?
2. What do you want people to do after seeing it?
3. What is the deadline, and is anything printing?
4. Who approves the design, and how many people?
5. Do you have a logo, brand colours, fonts, and photos — and are they usable quality?
6. What has worked before, and what has failed?
7. What is your budget range? (Ask. Guessing loses money in both directions.)
8. How many rounds of changes are included in what you're imagining?

## 4. Critique script

Use this on your own work and on other people's. It runs in four moves and takes three minutes.

1. **What is this for?** (State the purpose back. If you can't, that's the finding.)
2. **What do I notice?** (Facts only: sizes, alignment, contrast, count of colours, typefaces.)
3. **What works, and why?** (Tie to a mechanism: "the price is the only red element, so it isolates".)
4. **What fails against its purpose, and what would I test?** (Not "make it pop" — "raise body text contrast to pass 4.5:1".)

Rules: no adjectives about the person, no taste as evidence, and if you say something fails, you must name the mechanism.

## 5. Colour checklist

- [ ] Three colours maximum: **dominant** (60–70%), **support** (20–30%), **accent** (5–10%).
- [ ] Checked in greyscale — hierarchy still readable?
- [ ] Body text contrast at least **4.5:1**; large text (24 px+ or 19 px bold+) at least **3:1**.
- [ ] Checked with a contrast checker, not by eye. Free: WebAIM Contrast Checker.
- [ ] Not relying on colour alone to convey meaning (e.g. all "sold out" items in red).
- [ ] Palette survives the print method: a local digital press handles most things; a one-colour job does not.
- [ ] Palette works on the client's actual background — white, black, or a busy photo.

## 6. Type checklist

- [ ] Two typefaces maximum (one is often better).
- [ ] One family used at 2–3 weights is safer than two families.
- [ ] Body text: 16–18 px on screen, 9–11 pt in print; line height roughly 1.4–1.6× the size.
- [ ] Line length: 45–75 characters. On a phone, 30–40 characters per line.
- [ ] Headline tracking slightly tighter at large sizes; small caps or all-caps with slightly more tracking.
- [ ] No more than 3 type sizes in one piece.
- [ ] Nothing set in all-caps beyond 3 words (slow to read, and it shouts).

## 7. Export settings by destination

| Destination | Size / ratio | Format | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instagram feed | 1080 × 1080 (1:1) or 1080 × 1350 (4:5) | JPG or PNG | 4:5 takes more screen; keep text inside the central 80% |
| Instagram / TikTok story, Reel | 1080 × 1920 (9:16) | MP4 (motion) or PNG | Keep text out of the top 10% and bottom 15% |
| Facebook post | 1080 × 1350 | JPG | Link previews crop to 1.91:1 |
| WhatsApp status | 1080 × 1920 | JPG, under ~1 MB | Assume it will be viewed compressed |
| X / LinkedIn | 1200 × 675 (1.78:1) | JPG | Text must survive thumbnail size |
| Presentation slide | 1920 × 1080 | PNG per slide, or PDF | Export PDF for sending |
| Flyer / poster (print) | A5 / A4 at 300 dpi | PDF Print (with bleed) | See print specs below |
| Logo hand-off | Vector where possible | SVG or PDF | Plus PNG at 1000 px, and a white-on-transparent version |
| App / web screen | 390 × 844 (mobile) or 1440 × 900 (desktop) | PNG for review | Design at 1× and note the breakpoints |

**Rule:** export the largest sensible size, then let the platform shrink it. Never upscale.

## 8. Print specs (what to tell the printer)

| Term | Meaning | What to set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trim size** | The final cut size | A4 = 210 × 297 mm; A5 = 148 × 210 mm |
| **Bleed** | Extra image beyond the trim so cutting errors don't leave white edges | 3 mm on every side (Canva: "Show bleed" / PDF Print) |
| **Safe margin** | Keep text this far inside the trim | 5 mm minimum, 10 mm comfortable |
| **Resolution** | Pixels per inch at final size | 300 dpi for photos; 600+ for line art |
| **Colour mode** | How ink is described | CMYK for print, RGB for screen. Expect on-screen colour to shift |
| **Crop marks** | Guides for the guillotine | Included automatically in Canva's PDF Print |
| **Paper** | The single biggest quality signal on a cheap job | 130–170 gsm for flyers; 250–350 gsm for cards/posters |

If a job needs spot colour, foil, embossing or precise Pantone matching, it is not a phone job — take it to a designer with a laptop or to the print shop's own studio, and say so honestly.

## 10. Contrast quick reference

| Text | Minimum ratio | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body text | 4.5 : 1 | Anything you expect people to read in full |
| Large text (24 px+, or 19 px bold+) | 3 : 1 | Headlines |
| Icons and UI boundaries | 3 : 1 | Buttons, form outlines |
| Decorative | No minimum | If it carries no meaning, it's exempt — but be honest about what's decorative |

Test at webaim.org/resources/contrastchecker. Grey (#999) on white is 2.85:1 — the single most common accessibility failure in amateur design.

## 11. Free font pairings that work on a phone

| Heading | Body | Mood | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Playfair Display | Source Sans 3 | Editorial, premium | Big contrast; don't use Playfair below 20 px |
| Montserrat (bold) | Lato | Modern, commercial | Safe default for a small business |
| Archivo Black | Inter | Loud, poster-like | Headlines only; pair with a neutral body |
| DM Serif Display | DM Sans | Warm, boutique | Same superfamily, so it always agrees |
| Bebas Neue | Roboto | Event / sport | Bebas is all-caps only; never for body |
| IBM Plex Serif | IBM Plex Sans | Institutional, trustworthy | Excellent for reports and signage |

Rules: pair a *contrasting* style, not two similar ones. Two geometric sans-serifs together look like a mistake, not a decision.

## 12. Glossary

**Alignment** — arranging elements so their edges or centres share a common line.
**Ascender / descender** — the parts of a letter above the x-height (b, d) and below the baseline (p, y).
**Art direction** — the overall visual concept and the rules that make a set of images feel like one campaign.
**Aspect ratio** — width-to-height relationship, e.g. 1:1, 4:5, 16:9.
**Baseline** — the invisible line letterforms sit on.
**Bleed** — artwork extending past the trim edge so cutting errors don't show white.
**Brand** — the sum of what people expect from a business; identity is only its visible part.
**Brand sheet / style guide** — the document that fixes colours, type, logo usage and tone.
**Cap height** — height of a capital letter from baseline to top.
**CMYK** — cyan, magenta, yellow, key (black); print ink model.
**Complementary colours** — opposites on the colour wheel; high contrast, high energy.
**Composition** — arrangement of elements within the format.
**Contrast** — difference in size, value, colour, weight or texture; the engine of hierarchy.
**Copy** — the text, in designer-speak.
**Crop marks** — printer's guides showing where to cut.
**DPI / PPI** — dots/pixels per inch; resolution at final size.
**Eyeline** — the horizontal level of a subject's eyes in a photo; a very strong compositional line.
**Figure/ground** — the relationship between subject and background.
**Focal point** — the place the eye lands first.
**Font vs typeface** — a typeface is the design (Helvetica); a font is a file/weight of it (Helvetica Bold). Most people say font for both.
**Grid** — the underlying structure of columns and margins that governs placement.
**Hierarchy** — the ranked order in which the eye reads things.
**Hue / saturation / value** — the colour family, its intensity, and its lightness.
**Kerning** — spacing between individual letter pairs.
**Leading (line height)** — vertical distance between baselines.
**Letter-spacing / tracking** — spacing applied across a run of text.
**Lockup** — a fixed arrangement of logo + name + tagline.
**Logo vs mark vs wordmark** — a mark is the symbol, a wordmark is the name set in type, a logo is the whole thing.
**Logotype** — a wordmark.
**Mockup** — a realistic preview of the design in context (on a wall, a phone, a shirt).
**Negative space** — the empty area; also called white space.
**Optical alignment** — adjusting by eye because mathematical alignment looks wrong.
**Palette** — the defined set of colours for a brand or piece.
**Pantone (PMS)** — a standardised spot-colour matching system.
**Raster vs vector** — pixels vs mathematical paths; vectors scale without loss.
**Resolution** — amount of detail; pixels per inch at a given physical size.
**RGB** — red, green, blue; screen colour model.
**Rule** — a drawn line (also a principle, confusingly).
**Sans-serif / serif** — without or with the small strokes at letter ends.
**Saturation** — colour intensity.
**Scrim** — a semi-transparent layer behind text to guarantee contrast.
**Safe zone** — area guaranteed not to be cropped or overlaid.
**Spot colour** — a pre-mixed ink, printed separately.
**Style guide** — see brand sheet.
**Tileable / pattern** — a repeating unit designed to join seamlessly.
**Tracking** — see letter-spacing.
**Trim** — the final cut size of a printed piece.
**Typography** — the craft of setting type; broader than choosing a font.
**Value** — lightness/darkness.
**Vector** — see raster vs vector.
**Weight (type)** — thickness of strokes: light, regular, medium, bold, black.
**White space** — see negative space.
**X-height** — height of a lowercase x; a big x-height reads more clearly at small sizes.

## 13. Where to go after Day 15

In priority order:

1. **Do five real jobs**, even free or near-free, for real businesses. Real constraints teach faster than exercises. Charge something small — free work is treated as free work.
2. **Learn a desktop tool** when you can borrow an hour on a laptop: Affinity Photo/Designer (one-off purchase, no subscription) or Figma (free, browser-based, superb for UI). Figma is the single highest-value next skill if you want app/web work.
3. **Specialise.** Pick one: brand identity (small business work, repeatable), social content (steady retainer income), UI/UX (best paid, needs Figma), packaging (needs print relationships), or motion (CapCut → After Effects).
4. **Build a proper case-study portfolio** — three to five projects, each with the problem, process and result. Not a gallery of pretty images.
5. **Learn print production properly** with a real printer: ask to stand at the press for one job. It will change how you design forever.
