## Why this matters

Nobody hires a designer because they finished a course. They hire because they saw work that looked like the work they need, explained in a way they understood. Today you convert fifteen days of practice into the one thing that gets you paid: **a case study that shows how you think, on top of work that looks competent.**

This is also the day you become findable. A portfolio that exists only in your gallery is a secret. A single page with three projects, a price and a contact link is a business.

## Lesson

### 1. What a portfolio actually is

It is not a gallery of everything you've made. It is an argument, made to one specific kind of client, that you can solve their problem. Three projects that are clearly explained beat thirty images with no context, every time.

Choose your three:

1. **The capstone** — your deepest project (identity + applications + print + social).
2. **The one that shows range** — the most different thing you made (the campaign, the leaflet, the app screen).
3. **The one that shows judgement** — the hardest problem, the best writing, the piece where you said no to something. If you did the Day 14 client reply, that document belongs here.

If a project doesn't help that argument, leave it out. A weak fourth project lowers the average of the first three.

### 2. The case study structure

One page per project, five sections, 300–500 words total. Every section earns its place:

| Section | What goes in it | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The problem** | Who the client is, who their audience is, and the specific difficulty. No adjectives, no "passionate about". | 2–3 sentences |
| **The constraints** | Budget, format, deadline, print method, one colour, no photos — whatever made it hard | 2–4 bullets |
| **The thinking** | The concept and the *decision* you made: what you chose and what you rejected, and why | 3–5 sentences |
| **The process** | 3–5 images: thumbnails, wireframe, grid, the rejected concept, the test sheet | 3–5 images |
| **The outcome** | What exists now, what it does, what the client said. If you have no numbers, say what changed qualitatively — never invent statistics. | 2–3 sentences |

**Writing rules:** no jargon ("synergy", "disruptive"), no "I'm passionate", no list of software. Write like a person explaining a decision to another person. One strong sentence about a decision is worth a paragraph about your process.

Example of the difference:

- *Weak:* "I created a modern, vibrant identity that reflects the brand's values and target audience."
- *Strong:* "The shop opens at 6am for commuters, so the logo had to read from across a road in low light. I chose a heavy wordmark over an icon because an icon would have needed a caption to be understood."

The second one is hireable.

### 3. Process shots: show the thinking, not the mess

Use 3–5 images per project, chosen to prove a point:

- **The 50 thumbnails**, cropped to a grid — proves you generate before you settle.
- **The wireframe or grid** — proves you structure before you decorate.
- **A rejected concept** — proves judgement; label it "rejected because…".
- **The test sheet** (16 px, one colour, reversed) — proves you understand reproduction.
- **The final in context** (a mockup on a wall, a phone, a sign) — proves it works in the world.

Do not show every screenshot you took. Curation is the skill being demonstrated.

### 4. The portfolio page

You need **one page** that survives a phone. Use Canva's Website feature (free, publishes to a link), Notion, or a Behance profile. Content order:

1. **Who you are and what you do**, one sentence. "I design brand identities and social content for small businesses in Kampala." Not "creative designer passionate about design".
2. **Three projects**, each a card with one strong image and a title.
3. **One expanded case study** (the capstone) on its own page.
4. **What you offer** — three services, with a starting price or "from…" line. Yes, put prices. It filters enquiries and signals professionalism.
5. **One clear contact action** — WhatsApp link, email, or a form. One, not four.
6. **A testimonial**, if you have one. If not, ask for one today; a friendly client will write it.

Design it with the skills you just learned: three-level hierarchy, two typefaces at most, one accent colour, real margins, and nothing that requires pinching to read. Test on your own phone, in daylight, at arm's length.

### 5. Honesty about your level

You are fifteen days in. You are not a senior designer, and pretending otherwise loses work you could have won. Position yourself accurately:

- "I design brand identities and social content for small businesses" — true, and exactly what most local businesses need.
- Don't claim photography, motion graphics, packaging or web development experience you don't have.
- Do show the process — clients can see competence in a wireframe even if they can't judge a gradient.
- Say what you're learning next. Curiosity reads as professional; overclaiming reads as a risk.

The best first clients are the ones whose work matches what you can genuinely do well right now: a logo and a brand sheet, a flyer that prints correctly, a month of consistent social posts, a clean menu.

### 6. Launch, test, share

- **Test:** open the link on a phone that isn't yours (borrow one), in daylight, with a slow connection. Note anything you have to squint at.
- **Test the action:** send yourself a WhatsApp message through your own contact link. If it doesn't work, the portfolio doesn't work.
- **Share:** post it once, to your status, your story, and two local business groups. Message five people you already know who run businesses — not with "check out my portfolio", but with "I designed a brand for a shop like yours; here's the case study, happy to talk if it's useful."
- **Ask:** three specific people for one piece of feedback each, and one client for a testimonial.

### 7. The next 90 days

The course ends; the practice shouldn't. Write this down before you close the page:

| Week | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Two real jobs, even small. Charge something. Deliver the full package (brief → quote → work → handover). |
| 3–4 | Learn Figma properly on a laptop hour if you can borrow one. Redo your best screen at 1×. |
| 5–6 | Add one new case study. Ask both clients for testimonials. Raise your price by 20% for new enquiries. |
| 7–8 | Learn one adjacent skill: print production with a real printer, or basic motion in CapCut. |
| 9–10 | Build a template set you can sell or reuse: a brand sheet, five social posts, a flyer. |
| 11–12 | Review: which enquiries came in, which jobs paid best, what you avoided because you couldn't do it. Fix that gap. |

> **Try it (5 min):** write your one-sentence positioning. "I design ___ for ___ in ___." If you can't fill it in, that's your first task after the course, not a reason to wait.

## Guided practice

1. **(10 min) Choose three projects.** Write one line each on why it earns its place: depth, range, judgement.
2. **(20 min) Write the capstone case study.** Five sections, 300–500 words, no jargon. Do the writing first, in a note — not in the design app.
3. **(15 min) Select process images.** Five maximum. Label the rejected concept with the reason.
4. **(15 min) Build the portfolio page.** Who you are, three projects, one expanded case study, services with a "from" price, one contact action.
5. **(10 min) Mobile test.** Open it on another phone. Fix anything that requires squinting, pinching or scrolling sideways.
6. **(10 min) The ask.** Send one message to one real business owner with the case study link. Then ask one past client for a testimonial.

## Practical questions

1. Why is a case study more persuasive than a gallery of finished images?
2. What goes in "the outcome" if you have no numbers?
3. Why should you show a rejected concept?
4. What is wrong with the sentence "I'm a passionate creative designer who loves bringing brands to life"?
5. Why put prices on a portfolio page?
6. You have no real clients yet. What do you show, and how do you describe it honestly?

## Scenario

**The referral enquiry.** A friend's sister runs a bakery and messages you:

> "Hi, my sister said you do design. I need a logo for my bakery and maybe Instagram. How much and how long? I don't have a big budget but I want it to look professional, like the ones on Pinterest. I need it by next week because I'm launching."

Produce two things:

- **A reply** (under 200 words) that: asks the three questions that matter most, states a starting price and what it includes, gives an honest timeline, and sets a condition (a deposit to book the slot). It must be warm — this is a real person who trusts you — and it must not promise a Pinterest-level brand on an unknown budget.
- **A one-page profile** (PDF or image) you can send to any enquiry: what you do, three services with starting prices, what the process is (brief → concepts → refinement → handover), how long it takes, and how to pay. This is the document that saves you from rewriting the same message every week.

Then send the profile to one real person. Not hypothetically. Today.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Because finished images only show that you can produce something attractive, which anyone with a template can do. A case study shows that you can identify a problem, work within constraints, make and defend decisions, and deliver something that functions — which is what a client is actually buying and what they cannot get from a template. It also signals that you can communicate, which is half the job.
2. What now exists and what it does: "The shop now has a logo that reads at 16 px on a phone and on a 2 m banner, a brand sheet the sign-writer can follow, and eight social templates the owner can reuse without me." Then the client's own words, quoted. Never invent metrics; if you have a real one (sales, enquiries), use it with the source.
3. Because judgement is the thing clients are paying for, and a rejected concept demonstrates that you generated alternatives and chose deliberately rather than settling on the first idea. Label it honestly ("rejected: the icon became unreadable below 32 px") — it shows you test your work against real conditions.
4. It contains no information. It states a feeling the client cannot verify, uses the same words as every other portfolio, and doesn't say what you do, who for, or what you'll deliver. A client reading it learns nothing they can act on — and it signals that you'll be vague about scope and deadlines too.
5. Because price is the first thing most clients want to know, and hiding it means every enquiry becomes a negotiation before you've established value. A "from" price filters out mismatched enquiries, positions you in a range, and signals that you treat this as a business. It also stops the most common bad job: a client with one-fifth of your budget expecting full-service work.
6. Show the course work honestly: "Brand identity concept for a fictional bakery" or "Self-initiated project". Say what was real (the brief, the constraints, the printing) and what wasn't (the client). If you photographed a real shop with permission, say so. Then do two small real jobs in the next two weeks — a flyer for a friend's business, a logo for a church group — and replace the fictional work as fast as you can. Honesty plus momentum beats a fake client list, which is easily discovered.

## Research for tomorrow

You've finished the course. The research doesn't stop — it just becomes self-directed:

- **Figma, properly.** One hour on a borrowed laptop. Auto layout, components, styles. It's the highest-value next skill for paid work.
- **Print production, in person.** Ask a print shop to walk you through a job: file setup, proofing, paper, finishing. Then design something for actual press with their guidance.
- **A specialisation.** Pick one to go deep on: brand identity, social content systems, UI/UX, packaging, or motion. Follow five practitioners in that field and study their work weekly.
- **Business skills.** Read one book on freelancing (pricing, contracts, client management). Write your own simple contract.
- **A local design community.** Find one — a WhatsApp group, a studio that mentors, a meetup. Most paid work comes through people who have seen your work and trust you.
- **Teach one thing.** Explain visual hierarchy to someone in ten minutes. Teaching exposes the gaps in your understanding faster than another tutorial.

## Finish line

Fifteen days ago you had a phone. Now you have:

- A vocabulary that lets you diagnose design problems instead of guessing.
- A process: brief, research, thumbnail, build, test, present, hand over.
- A capstone identity with applications, print work, a campaign and a screen.
- The business documents: a rate card, a quote, a revision policy, a profile.
- A published case study and a portfolio page.
- A plan for the next ninety days.

That is a foundation, not a finished designer. The difference between people who get good and people who stay at day fifteen is simply whether they keep finishing work for real people. Go and find your first client this week — a small one, a cheap one, a real one.

**Come back to Day 3, Day 6 and Day 11 every month.** Composition, typesetting and art direction are the three skills that keep improving for a decade, and each pass will teach you something you couldn't see today.
