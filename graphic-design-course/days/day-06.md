## Why this matters

Yesterday you chose typefaces. Today you *set* text — the difference between typography as decoration and typography as a job. This is the day that changes how your work looks to anyone who knows design, because long-form setting has nowhere to hide: wrong measure, weak leading, orphaned words and arbitrary alignment all become obvious when there are 200 words on the page.

It is also the most employable skill in this course. Flyers and logos are competitive and cheap. Menus, newsletters, leaflets, reports, forms and brochures are constant, unglamorous, well-paid work — and almost all of it is a typesetting problem.

## By the end of today you can

- Set body text at a correct measure, leading and alignment for print and for screen.
- Use a pull quote, a drop cap and white space with restraint and a reason.
- Spot and fix widows, orphans, rivers and "runt" lines.
- Turn dense information into something a specific audience can actually use.

## Lesson

### 1. The grid: margins, columns, gutters

A page has three numbers that decide everything else:

- **Margin** — the white frame. On print, 10–15 mm for A4; on screen, 5–8% of the width.
- **Columns** — how many vertical bands the text sits in. Two or three for A4; one for a phone screen.
- **Gutter** — the space between columns. Usually 4–6 mm on print, roughly one line-height on screen.

Everything else — images, headlines, captions — either aligns to the columns or deliberately spans across them. That "spanning" is what makes a spread look designed: a headline that crosses all three columns, a photo that bleeds off one edge, a pull quote that occupies column three only.

**Set the grid first, in guides, before any content.** If you build content first, you will spend the rest of the day nudging things that almost align.

### 2. Body text: the four settings that matter

1. **Measure** — 45–75 characters. In a two-column A4, at 10 pt, that's usually 60–70 mm column width. In Canva, resize the text box, don't scale the text.
2. **Leading** — 1.4–1.6× for body at 9–11 pt. Add a little more if the typeface has long ascenders/descenders.
3. **Alignment** — left-aligned, ragged right. Justified text only if you control hyphenation and have a wide measure; on a phone, never.
4. **Contrast and weight** — dark grey (not light grey), regular weight, no all-caps, no italics for more than a phrase.

**Try it (4 min):** in Canva, put 150 words of real text into a box that's 60 mm wide at 10 pt, line height 1.5. Screenshot. Now widen the box to full page width. Screenshot. The first is readable; the second is a wall. This is the single most common print-design error in local work.

### 3. Widows, orphans, rivers, runts

- **Widow** — a single word (or very short last line) stranded at the end of a paragraph.
- **Orphan** — the first line of a paragraph stranded alone at the top of a column or page.
- **River** — a vertical gap of white space running down through several lines, usually caused by justification or double spaces.
- **Runt** — a very short last line, widowed or not.

Fixes: rephrase slightly (best), adjust tracking by a hair, adjust the text box height, or rewrite the sentence. Do **not** fix a widow by adding a manual line break that leaves a giant gap — that's more visible than the widow.

### 4. The editorial furniture

These are the elements that make long text look *published* rather than typed:

| Element | Job | Restraint rules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Deck / standfirst** | One or two lines that sell the piece before the body | Set 1.5× body size, lighter value, max 2 lines |
| **Pull quote** | A sentence lifted out, set large, often in a column or spanning one | Use once per spread; it must be a real sentence from the text |
| **Drop cap** | A large first letter | Only on the opening paragraph, only once |
| **Caption** | Explains an image | Smaller than body, grey, sits closer to its image than to the next one |
| **Byline / kicker** | Who and what | Small caps or tracked caps, above the headline |
| **Rule (line)** | Separates blocks | One or two; more starts to look like a form |
| **Sidebar** | Secondary info in a tinted box | One per spread |

The temptation is to use all of them at once. Pick **two** per spread. Restraint reads as confidence.

### 5. Designing for people who won't read it

Most real documents are not read; they are scanned by someone in a hurry, possibly in poor light, possibly with limited literacy in the language used. So:

- **Front-load.** Put the most important thing first, in the biggest type.
- **Chunk.** Short paragraphs, clear subheads, numbered steps.
- **Use icons as well as words** — never instead of words, unless the icon is universally understood (a phone, a clock).
- **One idea per line** where possible, especially in forms and instructions.
- **Test at the distance it will be used.** A leaflet on a clinic wall is read from 1.5 m; a menu is read at 40 cm; an A4 poster on a matatu window is read at 3 m in motion.

### 6. Phone-only production notes

Long documents on a phone are genuinely harder. Practical approach:

- Design **spreads and pages as separate files**, not one long document. Canva's A4/A5 page templates handle this well.
- Use **two columns maximum** on A4 in Canva on a phone — three columns becomes untouchable at that zoom.
- Turn on **guides and rulers**, and set the same margin value on every page, every time. Write the numbers in your log so you can't drift.
- Type in a separate note first, then paste. Editing 300 words inside a tiny text box on a phone is slower than writing it properly once.

## Guided practice

1. **(10 min) Set the grid.** In Canva, new A4 portrait. Guides at 12 mm margin, a gutter at the centre, and thirds across. Save as a reusable template.
2. **(15 min) Set real text.** Paste 200–250 words of real content (a menu with prices, a school newsletter, a product description). Two columns, 10 pt, line height 1.5, left-aligned. No headline yet. Read it on your phone from arm's length — fix anything you struggle to read.
3. **(10 min) Add the furniture, twice only.** Choose two of: deck, drop cap, pull quote, sidebar, caption, rule. Place them on the grid. Screenshot. Now add a third and screenshot again — most people will see that three is already too many.
4. **(10 min) Hunt the flaws.** Find every widow, orphan, runt and double space. Fix three of them by rephrasing, and write down what you changed. This is the exercise that separates typesetters from decorators.
5. **(10 min) Test the bleed.** Extend an image or colour block 3 mm past the page edge (Canva shows the bleed area). Then look at the preview: does the text stay 5 mm inside the trim? Add crop marks in the export dialog and check where they land.
6. **(10 min) Distance test.** Export as PDF, open it, and view it at thumbnail size on your phone. What is still readable? If the page has no visual structure at thumbnail size, a reader skimming a rack will never pick it up.

## Scenario

**The clinic leaflet.** A small health clinic needs an A5 leaflet explaining how to take a course of antibiotics correctly. The content they sent is 700 words of dense medical prose in long paragraphs, including dosages, side effects, when to return, and the clinic's hours.

The audience: patients collecting medication at the counter, some reading in a second language, some with limited literacy, many in a hurry, standing up, in poor light. The leaflet will be printed in one colour (black) on white A5, then also posted as a photo on the clinic's Facebook page.

Produce a one-page leaflet:

- Cut the 700 words to **no more than 220** without losing the four things a patient must know. Write the cut text first, in a note, before designing.
- Set a hierarchy that answers: *what is this · what do I do · how often · when to come back*.
- Use numbers as a design element (1, 2, 3 steps) rather than a paragraph.
- Include one icon or simple shape per step, drawn in Canva from primitives. No clip-art.
- One colour only. Prove it survives greyscale by definition.
- Leave the clinic's name, phone number and hours in a clear block at the bottom — the footer is functional, not decorative.

Then produce the social version: same hierarchy, square, type scaled up so it reads at thumbnail size in a feed.

## Answers and self-marking

1. Left-aligned text has a consistent starting edge, so the eye finds each new line without effort, and it tolerates imperfect hyphenation. Justified text is acceptable when the measure is wide enough (roughly 60+ characters), hyphenation is available, and the column is narrow enough to keep word spacing even — typically in newspapers and books with professional typesetting. On a phone or in Canva's auto-hyphenation-free setting, justification produces rivers.
2. The gutter is the space between columns. Too small and the columns visually merge, so the eye reads across into the next column's text; too large and the columns read as separate unrelated documents and the page feels disjointed. It should be clearly larger than the word space and usually a little smaller than the margin.
3. A widow is a short last line (often one word) at the end of a paragraph; an orphan is a first line stranded at the top of a page or column. Best fix for both: rephrase the sentence so the line fills or breaks differently. Second best: adjust the text box or tracking slightly. Never fix by inserting manual breaks that create a hole.
4. (a) Group and order the items (drinks / food / specials) with subheads and space, because a 45-item flat list can't be scanned; (b) align prices consistently — right-align them on a common axis or use dot leaders, so they form a readable column; (c) create one level of emphasis (e.g. the day's special or the best sellers) and set everything else at body size. Also valid: increase leading, remove boxes and borders, use one column if there's room.
5. Because a pull quote's job is to let a skimmer sample the *actual* argument, which builds trust; inventing a marketing line turns the page into an advertisement and duplicates the headline's job. It also creates a credibility problem if a reader reads the body and finds the quote isn't there.
6. Options: (1) reduce to a two-page leaflet with the extra content on the back — same print cost usually; (2) keep one page and cut hard to the four things the reader must act on, moving detail to a QR code or website; (3) keep the one page but make the extra content a separate handout, or run it as a series of social posts. Recommend one, explain the trade-off in terms of the reader standing at a counter, and let them decide. Do not silently shrink the type to fit.
