## Why this matters

Colour is the element beginners reach for first and understand last. Two things are true at once: colour is the fastest way to make something feel a certain way, and it is the fastest way to make something unreadable. Most amateur design fails not because the colours are ugly but because there are five of them, they all shout, and the text has vanished into them.

Today is not "the meaning of purple". It is: how to build a palette from nothing, how to keep it to three roles, how to prove it works before you commit, and how to survive printing and accessibility.

## By the end of today you can

- Explain hue, saturation and value, and adjust each independently in Canva or Coolors.
- Build a palette from a photo, and from a colour wheel harmony, in under three minutes.
- Assign 60/30/10 roles (dominant, support, accent) and defend which colour gets which job.
- Test any palette in greyscale and against a contrast checker before delivering.
- Avoid the five standard colour failures: too many hues, full saturation everywhere, grey text, colour-only meaning, RGB-only thinking.

## Lesson

### 1. Three dials, not one

Every colour can be described by three independent properties. Learn to move them separately and colour stops being mysterious.

- **Hue** — where it sits on the wheel: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple. Changing hue changes the *mood*.
- **Saturation** — how pure or greyed it is. High saturation shouts; low saturation whispers. Changing saturation changes the *volume*.
- **Value** — how light or dark. Changing value changes *readability and hierarchy*.

The most common beginner palette is five different hues at full saturation and mid value — maximum noise, minimum hierarchy. The professional move is usually: **one hue, varied in value and saturation**, plus one accent. That is why so many good brands look like "three blues and a red".

Try this in Canva's colour picker: choose one colour, then drag only the saturation slider to 50%, then only the value slider. You now have a family that agrees with itself.

### 2. Build a palette from a photograph

The fastest reliable method, and it always produces something that feels designed because a photograph already contains coherent colour:

1. Choose a photo with the mood you want (warm street scene, cool ocean, product shot).
2. In Canva, upload it and use **Colour palette from image** (in the colour picker, "Extract from image"), or in Coolors use the image picker.
3. You get five colours. **You will not use all five.** Pick: the darkest (role: text/dark), the lightest (role: background), one mid-tone (role: surfaces), one saturated colour (role: accent). Discard the rest.
4. Adjust: push the dark darker and the light lighter, because photos usually don't contain an extreme enough pair for text contrast.

This is how you build a palette that looks intentional in ten minutes, without a colour theory lecture.

### 3. Harmonies, and when each is right

| Harmony | How it's built | Feels like | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Monochromatic** | One hue, many values | Calm, confident, premium | Editorial, tech, luxury, anything minimal |
| **Analogous** | Two or three neighbours on the wheel | Harmonious, natural, soft | Wellness, food, lifestyle, illustration |
| **Complementary** | Two opposites | Energetic, loud, high contrast | Sales, sport, kids, calls to action |
| **Split-complementary** | One hue plus the two neighbours of its opposite | Energetic but less crude than complementary | Brands that want punch without cliché |
| **Triadic** | Three evenly spaced hues | Playful, vibrant, childlike | Toys, events, entertainment |
| **Neutral + accent** | Greys/beiges plus one saturated colour | Sophisticated, controlled | The safest professional default |

If you cannot choose, take **neutral + accent**. It is almost never wrong, and it is what most well-paid brand work actually uses.

### 4. The 60/30/10 rule

Assign jobs, not just colours:

- **60% dominant** — usually the background or the largest surface. Quiet. Light, desaturated, or a deep neutral.
- **30% support** — secondary surfaces, image blocks, body text.
- **10% accent** — buttons, prices, the one thing you want noticed. Accent is a spotlight; two spotlights cancel out.

In a poster that often means: cream background (60), photo plus dark text block (30), red price (10). If your accent colour covers a third of the page, it is no longer an accent.

### 5. Test before you commit

Three tests, two minutes total, and they catch nearly every colour disaster:

1. **Greyscale test.** Convert the design to black and white (Canva: filter, or export and use your phone's filter). If hierarchy survives, your value structure is right. If it becomes mush, your colours differ only in hue.
2. **Contrast test.** Put your text and background colours into the WebAIM contrast checker. Body text needs **4.5:1**, large text **3:1**. Grey `#999999` on white is 2.85:1 — fails, and is the most common failure in local design.
3. **Colour-blind test.** Roughly 1 in 12 men cannot separate red from green. Never use colour alone to carry meaning: label it, shape it, or icon it as well.

### 6. The five standard failures

1. **Too many hues.** More than three colours (neutrals excepted) is almost always noise.
2. **Everything at full saturation.** Nothing is loud if everything is loud. Desaturate the background, saturate the one thing that matters.
3. **Grey text on white or on photos.** Unless it's very dark grey on white, it's unreadable in sunlight.
4. **Colour as the only signal.** "Red means closed" fails for a colour-blind viewer and in a black-and-white print.
5. **Designing only in RGB.** Screen colour is emitted light; print is reflected ink. Saturated blues and greens often print duller or shift. If it will be printed, check the print preview and accept that a small shift is normal.

> **Try it (4 min):** open your Day 3 poster. Apply a neutral + accent palette: cream background, near-black text, one saturated accent on the price or the call to action. Screenshot. Compare with the original. The accent should feel like it's pointing.

### 7. A shortcut that beats guessing

Steal the *structure* of a good palette rather than the colours themselves. Find a brand whose mood matches your project; note which role each colour plays (dominant, support, accent, text); then substitute your own hues into those roles. Your palette inherits the logic that made theirs work, without copying their identity.

## Guided practice

1. **(10 min) Extract three palettes.** Pick three photos with different moods from your camera roll. Extract a palette from each in Canva. Save each as five swatches in a Canva page (use small rectangles, one per colour).
2. **(10 min) Assign roles.** For each palette write: dominant / support / accent / text / background. Delete at least one colour from each palette — the extraction always gives you one you didn't need.
3. **(10 min) Fix the extremes.** For each palette, darken the dark and lighten the light until you have a pair that passes 4.5:1 in the contrast checker. Record the hex values that pass.
4. **(10 min) Build one palette from scratch.** In Coolors, generate, then lock the colours you like and press space to evolve the rest. You're aiming for a palette you could use for a real business: three neutrals, two hues at most.
5. **(15 min) Recolour study.** Take your Day 3 poster. Duplicate the page three times and apply: (a) monochromatic, (b) complementary, (c) neutral + accent. Screenshot all three side by side.
6. **(10 min) Greyscale proof.** Convert all three to greyscale. One will survive best. Write one sentence explaining why, in terms of value contrast — not preference.

## Practical questions

1. Two palettes use the same three hues. One looks expensive, one looks cheap. What three properties could explain the difference?
2. Why does a photo-extracted palette usually need at least one manual adjustment?
3. What is wrong with using a bright yellow accent across 40% of a poster?
4. A client insists on red text on a green background for a Christmas flyer. Give a professional response and one concrete compromise.
5. Explain why "add more colour" is usually the wrong fix for a design that feels flat.
6. Your poster will be printed on a cheap CMYK digital press. Name two colours likely to disappoint and one way to reduce the risk.

## Scenario

**The boutique with a contradiction.** A women's clothing boutique wants a launch poster. Her brief: "I want it to look expensive, like the shops in the mall — but also friendly and welcoming, and I don't want black, black looks like a funeral. Also I want it to stand out on WhatsApp."

Constraints: one square post (1080×1080) and one A4 poster, phone-only production, printed in colour at a local shop, budget for one print run.

Produce **two versions of the square post**, with a one-paragraph rationale:

- **Version A — "expensive"**: neutral + accent, low saturation, generous space, one high-contrast element.
- **Version B — "friendly"**: analogous warm palette, softer value range, rounder shapes, more warmth.
- Then decide which one she should print and say why in one sentence, using the audience and the objective — not your taste.

Requirements: no pure black anywhere (use a deep warm brown or ink-blue), body text must pass 4.5:1, and both versions must survive the greyscale test.

## Answers and self-marking

1. **Saturation, value distance and proportion.** Expensive palettes tend to be desaturated, have a wide value range (very light background, very dark text), and use a tiny accent. Cheap-looking palettes tend to be fully saturated, sit in a narrow mid-value band, and spread several colours evenly. Same hues, entirely different restraint.
2. Because photographs usually contain no extreme light or dark — they're mid-tones, because that's what cameras expose for. Without a near-white and a near-black you cannot get text contrast or a clear hierarchy, so you must push the ends further than the photo does.
3. It stops being an accent. At 40% coverage it becomes a dominant colour that competes with every other element, and the eye has no single place to land. Accent works by scarcity — usually 5–10% of area.
4. Explain the problem, not the preference: "Red on green vibrates at the edges and about 1 in 12 men can't separate them, so the message gets lost — especially on a phone in sunlight." Compromise: keep the green as the large surface and use red only in small isolated areas (a badge, a ribbon) with a white outline or a dark band separating them; or switch the red to a warm pink/coral that reads as festive but separates in value. Give her the choice, with one recommendation.
5. Because flatness is a hierarchy problem, not a colour problem. More colours add more competing claims to attention, which makes it flatter still. The fix is value contrast, size contrast, and space — usually by *removing* things. Colour can then be used to reinforce the one element that survived.
6. Likely disappointments: saturated deep blues and vivid greens/purples, which often print duller or shift brownish on cheap presses. Risk reduction: choose a slightly lighter, less saturated version of the colour for print, request a physical proof for a short run, and keep critical contrast in value rather than in hue so a shift doesn't destroy legibility.
